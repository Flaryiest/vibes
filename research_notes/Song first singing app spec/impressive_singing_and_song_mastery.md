## 1. Listener perception: what drives lay listeners' judgments of singing quality?

### Takeaway
For non-expert listeners, the dominant audible criterion is whether the *intervals between notes* are the right size (relative pitch), not absolute key, and lay listeners are if anything stricter than experts. Beyond pitch, liking is driven by perceived fullness of tone (resonance) and how notes are started (attack); listeners are more forgiving of mistuning in a voice than in an instrument, and more so when there are lyrics, with a practical "noticed as wrong" zone starting around 20-50 cents.

### Cited Findings
- **[RESEARCH]** 18 lay listeners and 18 matched experts rated 166 a cappella "Happy Birthday" performances by untrained singers on a 9-point accuracy scale. For lay listeners, **only pitch interval deviation** significantly predicted ratings (model R² = .665); tonality modulations (key drift) and contour errors were not significant. For experts, interval deviation plus number of modulations explained about 81% of variance. — [Larrouy-Maestri et al. 2015, PLoS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0135394)
- **[RESEARCH]** In the same study, correlations of ratings with interval deviation were r ≈ .79 (lay) vs r ≈ .83 (experts); with modulations r ≈ .71 (lay) vs .81 (experts). Lay listeners rated *lower* (stricter) than experts overall (coefficient = -0.634, p < .001) and were more variable between judges. — [Larrouy-Maestri et al. 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0135394)
- **[RESEARCH]** Contour errors (going up when the tune goes down) correlated only weakly with ratings (lay r = 0.24), which the authors attribute to how rare contour errors were in the material; the study used one familiar song, so generalisation is limited. — [Larrouy-Maestri et al. 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0135394)
- **[RESEARCH]** Review of perceptual thresholds: some studies find listeners notice interval deviations of "around 10-45 cents"; others find tolerance "between 50 and 70 cents", and some beyond a semitone. Most used short sequences or only a final tone, so the role of musical context is unresolved. Listeners tolerate more mistuning in voices than in violin (Hutchins et al., 2012). — [Pfordresher & Larrouy-Maestri 2015, Frontiers in Human Neuroscience](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2015.00271/full)
- **[RESEARCH] (excerpt only)** Vocal generosity effect (Hutchins, Roquet & Peretz 2012, *Music Perception* 30(2), 147-159): listeners are less strict about tuning errors in sung tones than in instrumental tones. A later commentary summarises it as most listeners not noticing vocal tuning errors until about 50 cents, versus 20-30 cents for an instrument-like tone. I could not verify the 50-cent figure against the 2012 paper itself. — [BBS commentary on poor-pitch singing](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/music-production-deficits-and-social-bonding-the-case-of-poorpitch-singing/38A701E063D41ACB82558BB2CFAAFA8F)
- **[RESEARCH] (excerpt only)** Conference materials from the Larrouy-Maestri group report a lay "out of tune" threshold of about 20-30 cents (one poster: 27.45 cents, SD 10.45, 30 non-musicians) and about 10 cents for music experts, measured on melodic stimuli. — [BAPS 2014 poster](https://orbi.uliege.be/bitstream/2268/168480/1/Poster_Baps_2014.pdf); [ESCOM 2015 abstract](https://orbi.uliege.be/bitstream/2268/186233/3/ESCOM_Abstract.pdf); [2015 talk slides](https://orbi.uliege.be/bitstream/2268/188125/1/Goldsmith_presentation.pdf)
- **[RESEARCH] (excerpt only)** In familiar songs, detection of 50-cent pitch errors was worse when the rendition had lyrics, suggesting words distract from pitch errors. — [2022 study on pitch-error detection in familiar songs](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9751439)
- **[RESEARCH]** Note transitions matter beyond average pitch: in three experiments listeners rated 4-tone melodies whose third tone was correct or 50 cents sharp/flat, with scoops (glides) added at the start and/or end. Listeners were sensitive to the mistuning, scoops contributed independently of their effect on a note's average pitch, listeners were **more sensitive to scoops at the end of a tone than at the start**, and they did not necessarily prefer scoops that smooth continuity between notes. — [Larrouy-Maestri & Pfordresher, abstract (ORBi)](https://orbi.uliege.be/handle/2268/185325); published as [JEP:HPP 44(10), 1523-1541 (2018)](https://pure.mpg.de/pubman/item/item_2632642_1)
- **[RESEARCH]** What predicts *liking* of a singing voice: 326 online + 42 lab participants rated 96 a cappella excerpts by 16 trained female singers. Listener-rated perceptual features explained 43% of variance in liking; the largest contributors were **pitch accuracy, resonance (thin vs full) and attack (soft vs hard)**. Nearly 300 computed acoustic features explained "remarkably little" variance. Inter-rater agreement on liking was "very low" — taste is highly individual. — [Bruder et al. 2024, *Scientific Reports*, author summary](https://communities.springernature.com/posts/perceptual-but-not-acoustic-features-predict-singing-voice-preferences)
- **[RESEARCH]** Ratings by five professional-musician judges (not lay listeners) of 20 singers from poor to professional: intonation accuracy was "the highest contributing factor to the overall performance rating"; perceived vibrato correlated 0.910 with perceived intonation; volume was the weakest correlate. A linear model of seven rated dimensions reproduced the overall rating with r = 0.966: score = 0.161·Pitch + 0.189·Rhythm + 0.297·Vibrato − 0.27·Volume + 0.181·VoiceQuality + 0.115·Pronunciation + 0.311·PitchDynamicRange − 0.061. — [Gupta, Li & Wang 2018, APSIPA Transactions](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/apsipa-transactions-on-signal-and-information-processing/article/technical-framework-for-automatic-perceptual-evaluation-of-singing-quality/5F6AECB907FE842481D070850EDF1EFA)
- **[RESEARCH]** Tempo is a major driver of errors: occasional singers singing a familiar song at their natural tempo sang faster and made more pitch and time errors than professionals, but at a slow tempo most were as accurate as professionals; variance in proficiency was largely due to tempo. — [Dalla Bella, Giguère & Peretz 2007, JASA (PubMed abstract)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17348539/)
- **[RESEARCH]** Typical accuracy of ordinary singers: in a lab sample (N = 228 + 26), singers classed as accurate under a 50-cent criterion had a mean absolute deviation of 27.22 cents; those classed inaccurate averaged 173.87 cents. Whether someone counts as "inaccurate" swings from about 32% to about 59-60% depending on whether signed or absolute error is used. — [Pfordresher & Larrouy-Maestri 2015](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2015.00271/full)
- **[RESEARCH]** Accuracy (systematic bias, signed mean error) and precision (random variability) are distinct; mean absolute error mixes both. — [Pfordresher & Larrouy-Maestri 2015](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2015.00271/full)
- **[RESEARCH] (excerpt only)** When untrained listeners describe voices freely, they use categories covering sound quality, pitch changes, articulation and expressive variability; roughness and tension were the main features discriminating voices. — [Current Psychology 2022](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s12144-022-02734-7.pdf)

### Inferences
- Rough weighting for a friends-audience (inference from the above, not a published ranking): (1) correct interval sizes note-to-note, (2) staying in time with the track at a comfortable tempo, (3) full, unstrained tone, (4) clean note starts and tidy note ends, (5) expressive/dynamic variation; vibrato, riffs and diction polish come well after these.
- Because lay listeners key on intervals rather than tonal centre, a **wrong jump** (landing a whole step off after a leap) is more damaging than being uniformly a few cents flat. Slow key drift is less salient to lay listeners than to experts in a cappella singing — but with a backing track, drift becomes a constant clash against the accompaniment, so the a cappella finding should not be read as "drift doesn't matter" (inference).
- Lyrics and vocal timbre both buy tolerance: a target of "every sustained note within about ±25-30 cents, no note more than about 50 cents off" is a defensible "sounds in tune to friends" bar (inference combining the 20-30 cent lay threshold and the ~50 cent vocal-generosity figure).
- The scoop result implies the *ends* of notes deserve attention: a note that sags or slides off at the end is heard as out of tune even if its middle is right. Starts are more forgiving, which is why a small upward scoop into a note sounds stylistic while a droop at the end sounds weak (inference).
- Taste is idiosyncratic (very low inter-rater agreement on liking), so "impress friends" is best pursued by removing errors and strain rather than chasing one ideal timbre (inference).

### Gaps
- No study found that quantifies the relative weight of *timing* errors vs pitch errors in lay judgments of pop singing with a backing track.
- No direct lay-listener evidence found on the weight of confidence/stage presence, diction, or dynamics; PESnQ weights come from professional-musician judges on two songs.
- The exact per-experiment thresholds in Hutchins et al. 2012 were not verified (paper not accessible).
- "Talent" judgments specifically (as distinct from accuracy or liking) were not covered by any retrieved study.
- Bruder et al. used trained female singers on lyric-less melodies; transfer to an untrained low male voice singing pop is an assumption.

## 2. Song selection and keying for a low, untrained male voice

### Takeaway
Pick songs whose melody spans about an octave, sits in one register, and was (or can be re-keyed to be) centred in the comfortable middle of the voice; choose the key by where most of the melody and the chorus sit (tessitura), not by whether the single highest note is reachable. For a bass/baritone singing a tenor or female original, the standard move is to sing an octave below the original and then nudge the backing track up a few semitones, staying within about ±4 semitones of track shift to avoid audible artefacts.

### Cited Findings
- **[EXPERT]** Working range definitions: bass E2-E4, baritone G2-G4, tenor C3-C5. Tessitura ("the range in which you sound your best and feel most comfortable") matters more than highest and lowest notes. — [30 Day Singer, low voice types](https://30daysinger.com/blog/low-voice-types-bass-baritone-and-tenor-range)
- **[EXPERT]** A note can be inside your range and still be a poor fit, because the same pitch sounds different in chest, mix or head voice; ask whether you can reproduce the *sound*, not just hit the pitch. Beginners should prefer songs whose whole melody fits in one register, which is easier until mixed coordination develops. — [Camille van Niekerk, 30 Day Singer](https://30daysinger.com/blog/how-to-choose-a-good-song-for-your-voice-considering-range-registration-and-style)
- **[EXPERT]** High belts are demanding and beginners can hurt themselves pushing for them; options are a lower-key karaoke track (if the low notes survive), singing the high part in head voice as a style choice, or shelving the song. Beginners commonly struggle with songs by naturally high, trained singers (Adam Levine, Ariana Grande). — [van Niekerk, 30 Day Singer](https://30daysinger.com/blog/how-to-choose-a-good-song-for-your-voice-considering-range-registration-and-style)
- **[EXPERT]** Use "tonal models": artists whose range and tone you can easily recreate; explore their catalogues. Beginner-suitable songs span about one octave. — [van Niekerk, 30 Day Singer](https://30daysinger.com/blog/how-to-choose-a-good-song-for-your-voice-considering-range-registration-and-style)
- **[EXPERT]** Beginner-friendly songs typically span no more than an octave. — [eMastered beginner song guide](https://emastered.com/blog/easy-songs-for-beginner-singers)
- **[EXPERT]** Procedure for keying: find the melody's lowest and highest pitches, compare with your comfortable range; each semitone of key change moves the whole melody one semitone. Example given: "Perfect" (Ed Sheeran), original key A♭, range E♭3-A♭4; down 4 semitones gives E major, B2-E4. Original keys suit altos and tenors best; basses often find the top too high. — [30 Day Singer, how to change the key](https://30daysinger.com/blog/how-to-change-the-key-of-a-song)
- **[EXPERT]** Backing tracks on karaoke-version.com are limited to about ±4 semitones because "the audio begins to distort when you shift by a larger interval"; instrumental-only tracks tolerate shifting better than tracks with vocals. — [30 Day Singer, how to change the key](https://30daysinger.com/blog/how-to-change-the-key-of-a-song)
- **[EXPERT]** Octave trick for a very different voice type: shift the track *up* and sing an octave *lower*. Worked example: a bass shifts "Perfect" up to C (melody G3-C5) and sings it from G2 so the top note is C4. — [30 Day Singer, how to change the key](https://30daysinger.com/blog/how-to-change-the-key-of-a-song)
- **[EXPERT] (excerpt only)** Chorus test: sing the highest chorus line; if you strain or crack, lower the key; if it is so low you have no voice, raise it. If the chorus is tiring even though the extreme notes are reachable, the tessitura is the problem. — practitioner key-choice guides, e.g. [guitar-study-room](https://guitar-study-room.pages.dev/en/courses/sing-pick-key), [Worship Leader](https://worshipleader.com/articles/how-to-find-the-best-key-for-a-vocalist)
- **[RESEARCH]** Slow tempo sharply improves untrained singers' pitch and timing accuracy (see section 1), supporting slow-to-medium tempo songs. — [Dalla Bella et al. 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17348539/)
- **[EXPERT]** Low-voiced popular artists, baritones: George Ezra, Matt Berninger (The National), Beck, James Blake, Michael Bublé, Johnny Cash, John Fogerty, Mick Jagger, John Mayer, Anthony Kiedis, John Lennon, Dean Martin, Elvis Presley, Darius Rucker, Bruce Springsteen, Serj Tankian, Eddie Vedder, Randy Travis, Cat Stevens, Frank Sinatra. Basses: Leonard Cohen, Louis Armstrong, Melvin Franklin (Temptations), Avi Kaplan (Pentatonix), Michael McCary (Boyz II Men), Barry White, Josh Turner. — [30 Day Singer, low voice types](https://30daysinger.com/blog/low-voice-types-bass-baritone-and-tenor-range)
- **[EXPERT/ANECDOTE] (excerpt only)** Deep-voiced country singers: Josh Turner, Trace Adkins, Chris Stapleton, Merle Haggard. — [Hello Music Theory](https://hellomusictheory.com/learn/famous-country-singers-with-deep-voices/); [Musician Wave](https://www.musicianwave.com/10-country-singers-with-deep-voices/)
- **[ANECDOTE]** Voice-type labels in pop are inconsistent: singers "are primarily defined by their genre and their gender and not by their vocal range", and the same singer (e.g. Leonard Cohen) is called baritone and bass-baritone by different sources. — [Wikipedia, list of basses in non-classical music](https://www.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_basses_in_non-classical_music)
- **[ANECDOTE]** The Official Charts Company likened Gigi Perez's "Sailor Song" to Hozier, The National and Bon Iver (useful "sounds like" low-voice indie reference points). — [Wikipedia, Sailor Song](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sailor_Song_(Gigi_Perez_song))
- **[ANECDOTE — crowd database; octave errors occur, see section 9]** Song ranges listed in Singing Carrots' bass pop-rock chart (narrow, low-sitting examples): Bob Dylan "Make You Feel My Love" (G2-A♯3); Andrew Belle "In My Veins" (G2-C4); James Taylor "Fire and Rain" (G2-D4); Rufus Wainwright "Hallelujah" (G2-E4); Nick Cave "People Ain't No Good" (F2-C4); Beck "Jack-Ass" (A2-A3); Alphaville "Forever Young" (A2-C4); 10cc "I'm Not in Love" (A2-C4); Bruce Springsteen "Secret Garden" (B2-A3); Coldplay "Lost? (Acoustic)" (B2-E4); The Doors "Riders on the Storm" (D3-B3); Survivor "Eye of the Tiger" (C3-C4); Blue Öyster Cult "Don't Fear the Reaper" (C3-C4); Billy Joel "And So It Goes" (C3-C4); "Mad World" (Adam Lambert version, C3-D4); Eric Clapton "Layla" (D3-C4). — [Singing Carrots, pop-rock bass](https://singingcarrots.com/collections/pop-rock/bass)
- **[ANECDOTE — crowd database]** From the baritone charts: Frank Sinatra "Strangers in the Night" (A2-D4), "My Way" (A2-F4); Dean Martin "Memories Are Made of This" (A♯2-D4); "The Girl from Ipanema" (A♯2-C4); Billy Joel "Piano Man" (A2-G4); Radiohead "Exit Music (For a Film)" (B2-G4); Simple Minds "Don't You (Forget About Me)" (A♯2-F4); Lionel Richie "Hello" (E3-F4); Bill Withers "Ain't No Sunshine" (E3-G4); INXS "Never Tear Us Apart" (C3-A4); Bob Marley "Three Little Birds" (A3-A4). — [Singing Carrots, pop-rock baritone](https://singingcarrots.com/collections/pop-rock/baritone); [easy-listening baritone](https://singingcarrots.com/collections/easy-listening/baritone)

### Inferences
- Selection checklist (synthesis): melody span ≤ about 12-14 semitones; after keying, chorus peak sits 2-4 semitones *below* the highest comfortable note and the lowest note sits 2-3 semitones *above* the lowest comfortable note; tempo under roughly 100-110 BPM or with sparse lyric density; no long sustained note at the very top; original artist is a "tonal model" with a similar low voice where possible; the audience knows the song.
- Why low notes "vanish": the bottom few semitones of any voice are quiet and lack carrying power, so a full-octave drop that parks the verse on the floor of the range sounds mumbled even though the notes are technically hit. This is why "octave down, then up 2-4 semitones" usually beats a plain octave drop (inference consistent with the 30 Day Singer worked example; no acoustic study retrieved).
- Octave-down vs transposing: an octave drop keeps the original key (untouched backing track, harmonies still fit) but changes the vocal's relationship to the arrangement — a line written to soar above the band now sits inside it. Transposing by a few semitones keeps that relationship but is capped at about ±4 semitones of usable track shift. For a bass on a tenor/female original the realistic net shift is 7-10 semitones down, which is only reachable as "octave down + track up 2-5".
- Songs by low-voiced originals (Cash, Cohen, Berninger, Ezra, Hozier-adjacent indie) need little or no re-keying and let the learner use the original vocal as a like-for-like comparison target — the best case for the app's compare feature.
- The Singing Carrots figures are useful for shortlisting but must be re-measured from the isolated vocal in the app before committing to a key.

### Gaps
- No source retrieved for low-voiced artists in R&B or hip-hop-adjacent melodic styles; names here would be from memory and are omitted.
- No research found on "how far a song can be transposed before listeners think it sounds wrong"; the ±4 semitone figure is about audio artefacts in pitch-shifted tracks, not about musical perception.
- No published typical passaggio location for untrained bass/baritone voices was retrieved in this session; the "struggles above about D4-E4" assumption comes from the brief, not a source.
- Singing Carrots does not document how it assigns songs to voice types, and its ranges are user/database estimates.

## 3. Song-learning method: fastest evidence-based route to one performance-ready song

### Takeaway
The best-supported principle is that *how* errors are handled predicts next-day quality far better than time or repetitions: find the trouble spot immediately, slow it down, fix it, and repeat it correctly before moving on. Layering (rhythm and words separately from melody), chunking, spacing across days with sleep in between, and some interleaving are supported by a mix of small music studies and teacher consensus; none has been tested on beginner pop singers specifically.

### Cited Findings
- **[RESEARCH] (secondary summaries)** Duke, Simmons & Cash (2009, *J. Research in Music Education* 56(4), 310-321): 17 advanced pianists learned a 3-measure Shostakovich passage and were tested about 24 hours later. Practice time and repetition count did not distinguish the best performers; what did was how they handled mistakes — locating errors precisely, stopping before/at errors rather than playing through, slowing down, and repeating the corrected version until stable. — [UT Austin Center for Music Learning](https://cml.music.utexas.edu/online-resources/intelligent-music-practice); [research briefing summary](https://www.structural-learning.com/research-briefings/practise-the-hard-bit)
- **[RESEARCH]** Slow tempo: most occasional singers sang a familiar song as accurately as professionals when slowed down. — [Dalla Bella et al. 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17348539/)
- **[RESEARCH]** Interleaving: 10 clarinetists practised pieces either blocked (12 minutes per piece) or interleaved (alternating every 3 minutes). Where raters found a difference, interleaved pieces were rated better, but results were not uniform across the three raters; participants reported better goal-setting, focus and mistake identification when interleaving, yet most still preferred blocked practice. Very small sample. — [Carter & Grahn 2016, Frontiers in Psychology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4989027)
- **[RESEARCH] (excerpt only)** Spacing: Simmons (2012, JRME 59(4)) found fewer errors when practice sessions on a keyboard sequence were separated by 24 hours rather than 5 minutes or 6 hours. A separate piano study found no spacing benefit with very short intervals; reviewers call the music-spacing evidence inconclusive. — [Simmons 2012 record](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/distributed-practice-and-procedural-memory-consolidation-in-musicians-skill-learning/813362932149649409-5172); [Learning Scientists summary](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2017/11/09-1)
- **[RESEARCH] (secondary summary)** Sleep: Simmons & Duke (2006, JRME 54(3), 257-269) reported overnight, sleep-dependent improvement in performance of a newly learned keyboard melody — described as the first demonstration of consolidation-based enhancement in a specifically musical task. — [Bulletproof Musician summary](https://bulletproofmusician.com/heres-an-easy-way-to-help-your-brain-learn-faster/)
- **[RESEARCH] (secondary summary, old study)** Overlearning: Krueger (1929) found groups that kept practising past first success performed the same immediately but retained much more later, with more overlearning giving better retention. — [Didau summary](https://learningspy.co.uk/featured/should-students-be-overlearning/)
- **[RESEARCH]** Practice quantity still matters in aggregate: a meta-analysis found r = 0.44 between accumulated deliberate practice and musical achievement. — [Platz et al. 2014, Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00646/full)
- **[RESEARCH] (excerpt only)** Mental practice: Bernardi et al. (2013) are cited as showing mental practice improved movement velocity, timing and coordination in instrumental performance; reviews recommend it as a supplement to, not a replacement for, physical practice. No music-specific meta-analysis was found. — [glossary/taxonomy of mental practice in music](https://www.sciencegate.app/document/10.1177/1029864917715062)
- **[EXPERT]** Learn one layer at a time: "picking up an unfamiliar song and getting the words, rhythms, and melody right at the same time may take more than one session"; absorbing one detail at a time is faster than cramming all of it; break the song into manageable pieces. — [Pamelia Phillips, *Singing For Dummies*](https://www.dummies.com/article/academics-the-arts/music/voice/how-to-tackle-a-new-song-in-steps-177943/)
- **[EXPERT] (excerpt only)** A university voice-studio sequence: speak the text from memory; sing the words in rhythm on a single pitch; sing the melody on pure vowels (no words); then sing each phrase with words. — [Schoonmaker, Furman University](https://facweb.furman.edu/~bschoonmaker/learn.html)
- **[EXPERT] (excerpt only)** Speak the text in rhythm to settle pronunciation and word-rhythm before singing. — [voice-teacher blog](https://healthyandconfidentsingingvoice.blogspot.com/2019/09/learning-new-song-for-voice-lessons.html)
- **[EXPERT]** For non-readers, learn the melody by rote: listen and repeat. — [30 Day Singer, how to choose and work on a song](https://www.30daysinger.com/blog/how-to-choose-and-work-on-a-song)
- **[EXPERT]** "Sing and find out" loop for self-learners: learn the melody from the original artist, check pitches with a pitch-analysis app, record, and listen back. — [van Niekerk, 30 Day Singer](https://30daysinger.com/blog/how-to-choose-a-good-song-for-your-voice-considering-range-registration-and-style)
- **[RESEARCH]** People imitate recordings of *their own voice* more accurately than recordings of others, especially poor-pitch singers (see section 4) — relevant to using a corrected own-voice take as the practice target. — [Pfordresher & Mantell 2014](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Pfordresher&Mantell_2014_CogPsy.pdf)

### Inferences
Step-by-step method for one song (synthesis; each step tagged with the strength of its support):
1. **Key and map (day 1).** Measure comfortable range, set the key (section 2), split the song into phrases of one breath each, mark every breath point, and tag the 3-5 hardest phrases. [EXPERT]
2. **Listen-only passes.** Several attentive listens to the isolated vocal following the pitch trace before singing a note. [EXPERT; mental-practice evidence is indirect]
3. **Words in rhythm, no pitch.** Speak the lyrics in time with the track at 70-80% speed until they are automatic. [EXPERT]
4. **Melody without words.** Sing each phrase on a neutral syllable or lip trill against the pitch display, slow, until intervals are right. [EXPERT; slow-tempo benefit is RESEARCH]
5. **Combine, phrase by phrase.** Add words to the melody one phrase at a time; chain phrases into sections. Start with the hardest phrases and the chorus rather than always from the top, so the hard parts get the most repetitions. [EXPERT; backward chaining itself has no singing-specific evidence retrieved]
6. **Error rule (the Duke rule).** The moment a note is wrong, stop, isolate just that interval or bar, slow it, get it right, then require several consecutive correct repetitions before re-embedding it in the phrase. Never run the whole song over known errors. [RESEARCH, strongest single finding]
7. **Raise tempo gradually** to full speed only when the slow version is clean. [RESEARCH-supported in principle]
8. **Rotate, don't grind.** Within a session, rotate between 2-3 trouble spots every few minutes instead of 15 minutes on one. [RESEARCH, weak: n = 10]
9. **Daily short sessions with sleep between** rather than occasional long ones; expect yesterday's fixes to feel easier today. [RESEARCH, moderate, from keyboard tasks]
10. **Overlearn.** After the first clean full take, keep going for many more clean full takes across several days before calling it done. [RESEARCH, old; EXPERT]
11. **Record, compare, pick one fix.** Each session ends with one full recorded take, compared against the original; choose the single worst phrase as tomorrow's first target. [EXPERT]

- With 30-60 minutes a day, a reasonable split is about 60-70% on isolated trouble spots and 30-40% on full sections/run-throughs early on, inverting as the song stabilises (inference).

### Gaps
- No controlled study found on part-whole vs whole practice, backward chaining, neutral-syllable or lip-trill-first learning, or breath marking for singers; these rest on teacher consensus.
- Duke et al. 2009, Simmons & Duke 2006 and Simmons 2012 were read only via secondary summaries; all used keyboard tasks with trained musicians, not novice singers.
- The "50% overlearning" rule of thumb found on a piano-teaching site could not be traced to a primary study.
- No evidence retrieved on optimal number of consecutive correct repetitions.

## 4. Imitating the original artist

### Takeaway
Imitation of a recording is the default, teacher-endorsed way for a non-reader to learn melody, phrasing and style, but research shows people match a model best when it resembles their own voice, and teachers warn that copying a higher, raspier or heavily produced singer invites strain. Comparing pitch contour and timing against the original is useful for *what* notes and *when*; it should not be used to chase the original singer's timbre or register.

### Cited Findings
- **[RESEARCH]** Across experiments, both accurate and poor-pitch singers imitated recordings of *themselves* better than recordings of other singers, and the self-advantage was larger for poor-pitch singers. The effect was not explained by self-recognition, vocal timbre, or the absolute pitch of the targets. — [Pfordresher & Mantell 2014, *Cognitive Psychology* 70, 31-57](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Pfordresher&Mantell_2014_CogPsy.pdf)
- **[RESEARCH]** Imitating unfamiliar material is harder than matching single pitches: one study classed 58% of participants as inaccurate on single-pitch matching and 78% on novel-melody imitation. — Berkowska & Dalla Bella 2013, as reported in [Pfordresher & Larrouy-Maestri 2015](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2015.00271/full)
- **[RESEARCH]** For singing familiar songs from memory, relative pitch measures are the appropriate yardstick because people do not necessarily target the recording's absolute pitches. — [Pfordresher & Larrouy-Maestri 2015](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2015.00271/full)
- **[EXPERT]** Choose singers with a similar voice type as models ("tonal models"); singing along with similar voices helps stylisation and tone. — [van Niekerk, 30 Day Singer](https://30daysinger.com/blog/how-to-choose-a-good-song-for-your-voice-considering-range-registration-and-style); [30 Day Singer, low voice types](https://30daysinger.com/blog/low-voice-types-bass-baritone-and-tenor-range)
- **[EXPERT] (excerpt only)** Risks named by voice coaches: forcing notes above your range causes strain; heavy breathiness dries the folds; mimicking another singer's tone or range can create tension; copying a singer's sound may require distorting your own resonance; when imitating a record you are partly imitating the microphone and studio processing. Recommended use: imitation as a temporary exercise, not the sole method. — [Roger Love](https://rogerlove.com/singing-like-these-celebrities-can-hurt-your-voice/); [Tunedly](https://www.tunedly.com/blog/howtoproperlysinglikeanothersinger.html); [Classical Singer, "You vs. YouTube"](https://csmusic.net/content/articles/the-music-major-minute-15)
- **[RESEARCH]** Automatic reference-based scoring systems align the learner's pitch contour to a reference singer's with dynamic time warping and score distance in pitch, pitch-derivative, rhythm, timbre, vibrato and volume — the same comparison the app offers. — [Gupta, Li & Wang 2018](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/apsipa-transactions-on-signal-and-information-processing/article/technical-framework-for-automatic-perceptual-evaluation-of-singing-quality/5F6AECB907FE842481D070850EDF1EFA)

### Inferences
- Because the learner will usually sing an octave (plus a few semitones) away from the original, the comparison must be transposition- and octave-invariant: compare interval shapes, note timing and loudness contour, not absolute frequency (inference from the relative-pitch finding).
- The self-imitation advantage suggests a strong app feature: once a phrase is sung correctly (or pitch-corrected), let the learner practise against *his own* best take in his own key and octave rather than only against the original (inference; untested as a training method).
- Copy from the original: note choices, rhythm/phrasing, where breaths fall, where it gets louder and softer, word stress. Do not copy: register (falsetto vs chest), rasp/growl, breathiness, studio doubling. This split is my synthesis of the teacher warnings.
- Stylistic scoops and bends in the original will look like "errors" in a naive pitch comparison; given listeners' sensitivity to note endings, the app should weight the steady middle and the end of each note more than the first ~100 ms (inference from the scoop study; the exact window is a guess).

### Gaps
- No study found testing whether visual comparison against an original artist's pitch contour speeds song learning.
- No research found on vocal injury rates from imitation in amateurs; the risk statements are coach opinion.
- Pfordresher & Mantell full text (PDF) could not be opened; details come from the abstract.

## 5. Beyond hitting notes: what turns "in tune" into "impressive" within a few months

### Takeaway
The research-identified levers beyond pitch are full (not thin) tone, clean note starts, tidy note endings, rhythm consistency and some dynamic/pitch range within the performance; vibrato strongly co-varies with ratings among expert judges but is a marker of a developed voice rather than a quick win. For a beginner the highest return per hour is steadiness on sustained notes, on-time entries, breath-planned phrases and deliberate loud/soft contrast — vibrato and riffs should wait.

### Cited Findings
- **[RESEARCH]** Perceived pitch accuracy, resonance (thin vs full) and attack (soft vs hard) were the top three predictors of how much listeners liked a singing performance. — [Bruder et al. 2024, author summary](https://communities.springernature.com/posts/perceptual-but-not-acoustic-features-predict-singing-voice-preferences)
- **[RESEARCH]** In expert ratings, the weights on overall quality were largest for pitch dynamic range (0.311) and vibrato (0.297), then rhythm consistency (0.189), voice quality (0.181), intonation (0.161) and pronunciation (0.115); loudness carried a negative weight (−0.27). Intonation and vibrato ratings were almost collinear (r = 0.910), so the individual weights should not be over-read. — [Gupta, Li & Wang 2018](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/apsipa-transactions-on-signal-and-information-processing/article/technical-framework-for-automatic-perceptual-evaluation-of-singing-quality/5F6AECB907FE842481D070850EDF1EFA)
- **[RESEARCH]** Listeners are more sensitive to pitch glides at the *end* of a tone than at the beginning, and scoops affect in-tune judgments beyond their effect on average pitch. — [Larrouy-Maestri & Pfordresher (ORBi abstract)](https://orbi.uliege.be/handle/2268/185325)
- **[RESEARCH] (excerpt only)** Vibrato norms come mostly from Western classical singing: rate roughly 4-7 Hz (about 6 Hz average); rates below about 5 Hz are heard as too slow and above about 8 Hz as nervous. Operatic and jazz singers did not differ in vibrato rate, but operatic vibrato was wider, more regular and longer; classical benchmarks may not fit pop. — [Lester-Smith et al. 2021](https://www.colorado.edu/lab/motor-speech/sites/default/files/attached-files/lester_smith_2021.pdf); [Timbre and Orchestration resource](https://timbreandorchestration.org/tor/timbre-lingo/2020/6/27/vibrato); [open-access vibrato study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9510150)
- **[RESEARCH] (excerpt only)** Roughness and tension are the voice qualities untrained listeners most readily pick up when discriminating voices. — [Current Psychology 2022](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s12144-022-02734-7.pdf)
- **[EXPERT]** A pitch that is in range can still sound wrong if sung in the wrong register; beginners should stay in one register and avoid pushed high belts. — [van Niekerk, 30 Day Singer](https://30daysinger.com/blog/how-to-choose-a-good-song-for-your-voice-considering-range-registration-and-style)
- **[ANECDOTE]** The original "Sailor Song" vocal is three stacked takes panned left, centre and right — much of a record's vocal "size" is production, not a single voice. — [Wikipedia, Sailor Song](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sailor_Song_(Gigi_Perez_song))

### Inferences
Ranked by audible return per practice hour for this learner (my synthesis, not a published ranking):
1. **Right key, no strain** — removes tension/roughness, the qualities lay listeners detect most easily. Zero practice cost.
2. **Steady sustained notes with clean endings** — hold pitch flat to the end, release cleanly, no sag. Directly targets the end-of-note sensitivity finding.
3. **On-time, confident entries** — firm (not breathy, not scooped-from-far-below) starts on the beat; "attack" was a top-three liking predictor.
4. **Breath plan** — fixed breath points so phrases do not fade or get clipped; supports items 2 and 3.
5. **Two or three planned dynamic contrasts** (quiet verse, fuller chorus, one pulled-back line) — cheap proxy for "expressiveness" and for the pitch-dynamic-range dimension.
6. **Consistent tone** — same vowel shape and volume across a phrase; avoid dropping to a mumble on low notes.
7. **Diction** — only as far as the lyrics are intelligible; pronunciation had the smallest weight in expert ratings.
8. **Vibrato / riffs** — defer. Vibrato tracks overall skill in ratings but an uncontrolled wobble violates the steadiness goal; a straight, steady tone is stylistically normal in indie/pop. Revisit only after a song is otherwise solid (inference; no study on when beginners should start vibrato was found).
- Emotional delivery for a beginner is mostly achieved through items 3-5 plus knowing the words cold; no retrieved study isolates "emotion" for lay ratings of amateurs.

### Gaps
- **Microphone technique:** no source was retrieved in this session. Commonly taught basics (keep a consistent distance, pull back slightly on loud notes, sing across rather than directly into the capsule to reduce plosives, lower voices gain bass from close-miking) are unsourced here and should be verified before inclusion.
- No lay-listener study found on vibrato in pop, or on riffs/runs.
- No evidence retrieved quantifying how much stacked vocals/reverb in originals inflate perceived quality relative to a dry single take.
- The PESnQ negative weight on volume is unexplained in what I could read; it may be an artefact of collinearity in a 20-singer dataset.

## 6. Measurable features of a take and automatic singing-quality assessment

### Takeaway
Published scoring systems consistently find that pitch features computed against a time-aligned reference are the most predictive, followed by rhythm, with vibrato, timbre and loudness adding less; combining per-dimension scores ("late fusion") tracks human overall ratings at about r = 0.9 on small datasets. Research gives usable pitch thresholds (about 25-30 cents mean absolute error for "accurate", 50 cents as the standard note-level pass/fail line) but no validated millisecond tolerance for sung onsets against a backing track.

### Cited Findings
- **[RESEARCH]** PESnQ framework: reference-based features are pitch-contour DTW distance, pitch-derivative and median-subtracted pitch distances (key-invariant intonation), rhythm features (deviation of the DTW alignment path from a straight line, on pitch and on MFCCs), timbral distance (MFCC DTW, for voice quality/pronunciation), vibrato segment distance (likeliness, rate, extent), volume distance (short-term log energy DTW) and pitch dynamic range distance. — [Gupta, Li & Wang 2018](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/apsipa-transactions-on-signal-and-information-processing/article/technical-framework-for-automatic-perceptual-evaluation-of-singing-quality/5F6AECB907FE842481D070850EDF1EFA)
- **[RESEARCH]** PESnQ results: each perceptual dimension was predicted at song level with correlation above 0.7; overall score correlation with human judges was 0.725 with early fusion and 0.904 with late fusion (predict each dimension, then apply the human-derived equation); utterance-level correlation 0.59. Human inter-judge correlation on overall quality was 0.82 (upper bound in leave-one-judge-out 0.87). Dataset: 20 singers, 2 songs, about 120 twenty-second utterances, 5 musician judges. — [Gupta, Li & Wang 2018](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/apsipa-transactions-on-signal-and-information-processing/article/technical-framework-for-automatic-perceptual-evaluation-of-singing-quality/5F6AECB907FE842481D070850EDF1EFA)
- **[RESEARCH] (excerpt only)** Reference-free scoring: Nakano, Goto & Hiraga (Interspeech 2006) classified singing as good/poor with 83.5% accuracy over 600 sequences using only "pitch interval accuracy" (how well the F0 trajectory fits a 100-cent semitone grid) and vibrato features, without knowing the melody. — [Nakano et al. 2006](https://www.isca-archive.org/interspeech_2006/nakano06_interspeech.html)
- **[RESEARCH] (excerpt only)** Limitation of grid-fit scoring: a singer holding one note throughout would score well, because the measure ignores the melody. Later reference-free work ranks singers with music-theory-based pitch-histogram measures instead. — [Gupta, Li & Wang 2019, IEEE/ACM TASLP](https://smcnus.comp.nus.edu.sg/archive/pdf/2019-2021/2019_Chitra_TASLP_08871113.pdf)
- **[RESEARCH] (excerpt only)** Molina et al. combined intonation and rhythm similarity measures from DTW-aligned F0 contours via polynomial regression to fit the judgments of 4 trained musicians on 27 performances; DTW-based measures were described as simple and effective for both intonation and rhythm. — [Molina 2012 thesis](https://emilio-molina.github.io/publications/Molina-2012-Automatic-scoring-of-singing-voice-based-on-melodic-similarity-measures.pdf)
- **[RESEARCH] (excerpt only)** Caveat: aligning test and reference by pitch contour makes the rhythm score depend on pitch correctness, which is why PESnQ adds MFCC-based alignment for rhythm. — [Gupta et al. 2017, PESnQ conference paper](https://smcnus.comp.nus.edu.sg/archive/pdf/2017-2018/2017_Perceptual_Evaluation_of_Singing_Quality.pdf)
- **[RESEARCH]** Research conventions for pitch scoring: ±50 cents is the common criterion for calling a sung note accurate; singers passing it averaged 27 cents mean absolute deviation; signed mean error measures bias (accuracy), spread measures precision; interval-based (relative) measures are preferred for familiar songs. — [Pfordresher & Larrouy-Maestri 2015](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2015.00271/full)
- **[RESEARCH]** Lay listeners' ratings track mean absolute *interval* deviation from equal temperament (R² = .665 on its own). — [Larrouy-Maestri et al. 2015](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0135394)
- **[RESEARCH] (excerpt only)** Timing perception, general auditory evidence only: trained listeners need about 15-20 ms to judge the order of two tones; average synchrony-discrimination thresholds are around 10 ms in easy conditions; just-noticeable asynchronies in groove contexts are "a few tens of milliseconds" and lower for musicians; in recorded singing ensembles, small asynchrony differences across rehearsals were not perceived by listeners. — [D'Amario, Daffern & Bailes 2019, PLoS ONE](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6561579); [JND thresholds in a groove context](https://www.ebsco.com/articles/physics/95de1f13-1cbb-5d38-97a7-5e0eec67f036/just-noticeable-difference-thresholds-of-asynchrony-and-non-isochrony-in-a-multi-instrumental-groove-based-context)
- **[RESEARCH] (excerpt only)** Vibrato reference values: rate about 4-7 Hz, extent on the order of a semitone in classical singing; <5 Hz heard as slow, >8 Hz as nervous. — [Lester-Smith et al. 2021](https://www.colorado.edu/lab/motor-speech/sites/default/files/attached-files/lester_smith_2021.pdf)

### Inferences
Suggested per-take metrics and provisional targets (the thresholds marked "inferred" are my extrapolations, to be tuned on real takes):

| Metric (per note unless stated) | How to compute | Provisional target | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Note pitch error, key/octave-normalised | median F0 of the steady portion vs reference note, after removing the take's overall offset | ≤ 25-30 cents mean absolute; flag notes > 50 cents | research (27-cent mean for accurate singers; 20-30 cent lay threshold; 50-cent criterion) |
| Interval error | sung interval minus reference interval between consecutive notes | mean absolute ≤ about 30 cents; flag > 50 | research (best lay predictor); threshold inferred |
| % notes in tune | share of notes within ±50 cents | > 90% for "impressive", > 80% "fine" | criterion from research; percentages inferred |
| Within-note stability | SD of F0 over the steady portion; separate end-of-note slope | SD < about 20-25 cents; end drift < about 30 cents | inferred from scoop study; no published norm found |
| Overall bias | signed mean error across the take | within ±15-20 cents | accuracy/precision distinction; threshold inferred |
| Key drift | slope of signed error over time | less than about 30 cents across the song | inferred |
| Onset timing | sung onset minus reference onset (after allowing a constant style offset) | most onsets within about ±50-80 ms; flag > 100-120 ms | **inferred — no singing-specific study found**; perceptual floors are 10-30 ms |
| Loudness contour similarity | correlation of smoothed energy envelopes after alignment | higher is better; no norm | PESnQ volume feature |
| Dynamic range | loudest minus quietest phrase level | present and intentional | PESnQ pitch/dynamics dimension; inferred |
| Vibrato (if any) | rate and extent on sustained notes | 5-7 Hz, regular; otherwise prefer straight tone | classical norms |

- Score dimensions separately and show the worst one, rather than a single blended number: late fusion beat early fusion, and dimension-level feedback is what the PESnQ authors argue helps learners.
- Use MFCC/energy-based onset alignment for timing, not pitch-based alignment, so a wrong-pitch-but-on-time note is not double-penalised.
- Because lay listeners are generous to voices with lyrics, the scoring bar can be more lenient than an instrument tuner's; a visual "green zone" of ±50 cents with a tighter ±25 "gold zone" matches the evidence.

### Gaps
- No study retrieved gives a listener tolerance in milliseconds for a solo vocal against a backing track, or separates "laid-back" stylistic lateness from error.
- No published norm for within-note pitch stability (cents SD) in pop singing was found.
- PESnQ's exact per-dimension correlation table and Molina's correlation values are in figures/PDFs I could not read.
- All automatic-assessment datasets found are small (20-27 performances) and judged by musicians, not lay listeners; commercial karaoke scoring algorithms are not documented in the retrieved literature.

## 7. Performance readiness: from good private takes to singing in front of people

### Takeaway
The transferable evidence is that practising under realistic performance conditions reproduces the physiological stress of the real thing and is rated useful by performers, and that anxiety is better reframed than suppressed; teachers add a graded-exposure ladder (record, video, one friend, small group) and insist on over-rehearsal so the song survives adrenaline. No source gave a credible number for the practice-room-to-audience drop.

### Cited Findings
- **[RESEARCH]** Simulated performance: violinists showed comparable state anxiety and heart-rate-variability patterns in a simulated audition and a real audition, and rated the simulations as realistic and useful for developing performance skills. Sample was seven players. — [Williamon, Aufegger & Eiholzer 2014, Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00025/full)
- **[RESEARCH] (excerpt only)** Effects of simulation training on anxiety are mixed: one study found reduced state anxiety versus control, especially in high-trait-anxiety musicians; another found no consistent change in self-reported anxiety or heart rate. The motivating problem is that musicians rehearse in rooms and conditions very unlike those they perform in. — [Williamon et al. 2014](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00025/full); [Waddell, Bland & Williamon 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1694986/full)
- **[EXPERT] (excerpt only)** Start by singing for friends, with a song that feels easy and fun; tell them whether you want feedback or just the experience. — [30 Day Singer, stage fright](https://30daysinger.com/blog/stage-fright); [Songbird SF](https://songbirdsf.com/five-tips-for-singing-in-front-of-a-crowd/)
- **[EXPERT] (excerpt only)** The first few performances are the hardest and each gets easier; nerves speed up breathing, so deliberately slow it; the body goes into fight-or-flight when singing in front of people. — [Last Minute Musicians](https://www.lastminutemusicians.com/blog/dealing-with-anxiety-and-stage-fright-as-a-musician/); [ChoralNet](https://choralnet.org/archives/532209)
- **[EXPERT, citing research] (excerpt only)** Performers who reframe nerves as excitement tend to do better than those who try to calm down or suppress them. — [Last Minute Musicians](https://www.lastminutemusicians.com/blog/dealing-with-anxiety-and-stage-fright-as-a-musician/)
- **[EXPERT] (excerpt only)** Practising a song until it can be performed without thinking is the main protection against nerves. — [stage-fright course notes](https://guitar-study-room.pages.dev/en/courses/stage-fright)
- **[RESEARCH]** Lyrics and vocal timbre make listeners more tolerant of mistuning, and a backing track at a comfortable tempo supports accuracy (sections 1 and 2) — conditions that favour karaoke-style performance over exposed a cappella singing. — [Pfordresher & Larrouy-Maestri 2015](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2015.00271/full); [Dalla Bella et al. 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17348539/)

### Inferences
- Readiness ladder (synthesis): (1) full takes from memory with no pitch display; (2) one-take rule — record the first take of the day and judge only that; (3) video takes standing, as if performing; (4) sing for one trusted person; (5) two or three people; (6) karaoke/party. Move up when the *first* take at the current rung meets the section 6 targets.
- Remove the scaffolding before performing: the real-time pitch display and the original's guide vocal will not be there. Practise with backing track only, at the speaker volume and position of a real setting.
- Plan for a drop: under adrenaline, tempo rushes, breath shortens and the top notes are the first to fail. Keying the song a semitone or two lower than the "best private take" key and over-rehearsing the first line (the entry sets the key and the audience's impression) are cheap insurance (inference; teachers commonly advise performing material well inside one's limits, but I retrieved no source with a figure).
- With a backing track is the right default for a first performance: it fixes key and tempo and masks small errors. A cappella removes the key reference, and although lay listeners penalise drift less a cappella, starting on the wrong note is a common failure — if singing a cappella, get a starting pitch from a phone.
- Memorisation: speaking the lyrics from memory at tempo (section 3, step 3) is the test; lyric failure is the most visible way a performance collapses (inference).

### Gaps
- No source quantified the typical gap between practice-room and in-front-of-people quality; claims such as "you lose X%" were not found and should not be stated.
- The excitement-reappraisal finding is, to my knowledge, from Brooks (2014, *J. Experimental Psychology: General*), which used a karaoke singing task; I did not retrieve it in this session, so its figures are not reported here.
- No research found on first-time adult singers' performance anxiety specifically, or on video self-recording as an exposure method.
- No evidence retrieved comparing karaoke, backing-track and a cappella performance for beginners.

## 8. Timelines

### Takeaway
There is no research on time-to-one-impressive-song; teacher and learner reports cluster around "weeks to a couple of months of daily work for one manageable song", with range fit being the main determinant. Research supports optimism about the starting point — most untrained adults can already sing a familiar tune roughly in tune when they slow down — but a minority (roughly 15%, estimates vary widely) start with a genuine pitch-matching problem that lengthens everything.

### Cited Findings
- **[RESEARCH]** Most occasional singers can sing a familiar song in tune and in time at a slow tempo; only a minority made numerous pitch errors. — [Dalla Bella et al. 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17348539/)
- **[RESEARCH]** Prevalence of poor-pitch singing is often put at about 15% (Pfordresher & Brown 2007), but estimates "may range from 2 to 78%" depending on task and measure. — [Pfordresher & Larrouy-Maestri 2015](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2015.00271/full)
- **[ANECDOTE] (excerpt only)** A singer on a vocal-academy forum reports needing about two months of daily repetition to fully "own" a song; a moderator replies that it depends on the song — wide-ranging songs or songs near the top of the range take longer. — [Ken Tamplin Vocal Academy forum](https://forum.kentamplinvocalacademy.com/discussion/comment/26565/)
- **[EXPERT/ANECDOTE] (excerpt only)** A voice-teacher blog estimates "a few months" of steady practice to get the basics; practising about an hour a week takes longer. — [Yona Marie Music](https://yonamariemusic.com/yona/blog/89/how-long-does-it-take-to-get-good-at-singing)
- **[EXPERT] (excerpt only)** Professional singers can take many months before a song is truly bedded in. — [choir-leader blog](https://choirplace.com/blogs/78/1156/how-long-does-it-take-to-learn-a)
- **[EXPERT] (excerpt only)** One-to-one teaching "can speed things along a lot" because technique is corrected in real time. — [voice-studio blog](https://kimberley-smith-krfc.squarespace.com/blog/how-long-does-it-take-to-learn-to-sing)
- **[RESEARCH]** Next-day performance quality depends on practice strategy more than minutes practised (section 3). — [UT Austin Center for Music Learning](https://cml.music.utexas.edu/online-resources/intelligent-music-practice)

### Inferences
Planning estimates for this learner (30-60 min/day, real-time feedback, well-keyed song). These are my extrapolations from the above and are **not** published figures:
- **Days 1-3:** key chosen, lyrics spoken in rhythm from memory, melody of chorus roughly right at slow tempo.
- **Weeks 1-2:** whole song sung through at full tempo with the track; recognisable, mostly in tune, with known trouble phrases.
- **Weeks 3-6:** trouble phrases fixed; first takes reliably hit the section 6 targets; "friends would say that's actually good" level for a narrow-range, well-keyed song.
- **Weeks 6-10:** performance-proofed (memory, no display, in front of people). Second and third songs go faster — expect roughly half the time — because pitch control, breath planning and the practice routine carry over.
- **Faster if:** song spans about an octave and sits mid-range after keying; slow/medium tempo; learner already matches pitch reasonably on day 1; daily practice with the stop-and-fix error rule; low-voiced original to model.
- **Slower if:** baseline pitch matching is poor (the ~15% group — detectable in the first session from note-level error well above 50-100 cents); the song needs notes near the top of the range; fast, wordy delivery; practice is mostly full run-throughs; voice still settling at 18 (inference).

### Gaps
- No controlled or survey data found on time for an adult beginner to bring one song to a listener-rated standard.
- The Musora "month-by-month timeline" and similar teacher pages surfaced in search but were not read.
- No data on how much real-time visual feedback shortens song-learning time specifically (general feedback research was excluded from this brief).

## 9. The learner's three target songs: facts, keying and difficulty

### Takeaway
All three originals sit far above an untrained bass/baritone at pitch, so each should be sung about an octave below the original and then adjusted by a few semitones; "Sailor Song" is a sensible first choice (one-octave range, 95 BPM, 4/4, acoustic, no rap), "Shape of You" is the easiest fit for a low voice in pitch terms but is rhythm- and lyric-dense, and "Beauty and a Beat" is the poorest fit (tenor-range hook, 128 BPM EDM, rap verse). Published range figures disagree by whole octaves between sites, so every range below must be re-measured from the isolated vocal in the app.

### Cited Findings

**Song 1 — Gigi Perez, "Sailor Song" (2024)**
- **[Sheet-music publisher]** Original published key **B major**; time signature **4/4**; tempo marking "Moderate Folk", **quarter note = 95**; vocal range **G♯4-G♯5** (exactly one octave); scored for voice and piano. — [Musicnotes MN0295089](https://www.musicnotes.com/sheetmusic/gigi-perez/sailor-song/MN0295089)

- **[ANECDOTE — crowd transcription] (excerpt only)** Hooktheory also gives B major. A different Musicnotes arrangement in B♭ major is marked quarter note = 120, so tempo markings differ between arrangements. — [Hooktheory, Sailor Song](https://hooktheory.com/theorytab/view/gigi-perez/sailor-song); [Musicnotes MN0300127](https://api.musicnotes.com/sheetmusic/gigi-perez/sailor-song/MN0300127)
- **[Encyclopedia, citing Billboard / Official Charts]** An "acoustic ballad", 3:31 long, written on guitar; Perez recorded three vocal tracks panned left, right and centre; its sound was compared to Hozier, The National and Bon Iver. ### Inferences
Everything in this subsection is my own reasoning. Assumed learner range: comfortable E2/G2 up to D4/E4, to be measured. "Net shift" = semitones relative to the original sounding vocal.

**Octave caveats (important)**
- *Sailor Song:* the published G♯4-G♯5 would put the verse in a high-soprano area, which does not match a recording likened to Hozier and The National. I infer the **sounding** lead is most likely **G♯3-G♯4** (sheet notated an octave above sounding pitch), with the low, speech-like verse around B3-D♯4 and the belted chorus reaching F♯4-G♯4. Unverified — measure from the isolated vocal. Either way the song spans one octave, G♯ to G♯.
- *Shape of You:* I infer Sheeran's sounding lead is **G♯3-B4** (Musicnotes), with most of the melody on a few pentatonic notes around B3-F♯4; the Singing Carrots G♯2-B3 figure is an octave too low for a tenor, and Wikipedia's G♯5 top most likely counts falsetto backing lines. Unverified.
- *Beauty and a Beat:* I infer the lead tessitura is **B3-G4** with peaks to A4 (Hooktheory sections + Wikipedia top note); the D3 low and G5 high probably come from the intro/ad-libs or non-lead lines.
- Voice types: none of the retrieved sources states a voice type for any of the three singers. Bieber and Sheeran are commonly described as tenors, and Perez as a notably low-voiced female singer; treat all three as **unsourced** here.
- Falsetto/rap: sources confirm only the Nicki Minaj rap verse in "Beauty and a Beat". From general familiarity (unsourced): "Sailor Song" has no rap and no required falsetto; "Shape of You" has no rap but a rapid, near-spoken verse delivery and high layered backing vocals; "Beauty and a Beat" has high ad-libs that Bieber may take in head voice.

- Selection rule to apply after measuring his range: choose the shift where the chorus's highest repeated note is 2-4 semitones below his highest comfortable note and the song's lowest note is at least 2-3 semitones above his lowest comfortable note; when both cannot be satisfied, protect the top.
- App implication: comparison against the original isolated vocal must be done at the net shift (e.g. −9 semitones), i.e. octave- and transposition-invariant.

**Hardest passages (inference from the figures above plus general familiarity; verify by ear)**
- *Sailor Song:* (1) the chorus — the highest part of the octave, sung loud, with long lines back to back and little room to breathe, and sustained notes at phrase ends; (2) the jump in register and intensity from the low, quiet verse to the chorus; (3) the lowest verse notes if keyed too low; (4) exposure — a sparse acoustic arrangement masks nothing, and a single dry voice will sound thinner than the original's three stacked takes.
- *Beauty and a Beat:* (1) the chorus hook, repeated many times at the top of the tessitura; (2) the A4-equivalent peaks/ad-libs; (3) 128-BPM syncopated verse and pre-chorus delivery with short breath gaps; (4) the Nicki Minaj rap verse — needs a decision: rap it (rhythm and diction at speed, no pitch demands), skip it with an edited track, or hand it to a friend; (5) the song relies on production energy, so a low solo voice has less to "impress" with.
- *Shape of You:* (1) rhythmic precision and diction in the fast, wordy, syncopated verses, especially verse 2, with few breath points; (2) the pre-chorus and "Oh-I-oh-I" chant, which climb to the top of the range; (3) the high layered backing lines (leave them to the track); (4) the lowest notes (G♯2 region) if sung a plain octave down.

**Is "Sailor Song" a sensible first song? Yes, with two cautions.**
- For: one-octave range (meets the teacher "about an octave" beginner criterion); 95 BPM in 4/4 (slow-to-moderate tempo is where untrained singers are most accurate); no rap, no fast patter, no confirmed falsetto; simple, repetitive, strophic structure; low-voice aesthetic that survives being sung by a deep male voice; current and recognisable, with an emotional chorus that gives an "impressive" payoff.
- Cautions: the chorus is the make-or-break section (highest, loudest, least breath), so keying must be decided by the chorus test, not the verse; and the bare arrangement exposes pitch and tone more than a dense pop track would. Both are manageable with the key choice above and chorus-first practice.
- Order comment: on pitch-fit and rhythm grounds, "Shape of You" would be a gentler second song than "Beauty and a Beat" (narrow melody, plain octave-down fit, unshifted track), leaving the fast EDM song with the rap section for last. The learner's stated order still works; this is only a difficulty observation.
