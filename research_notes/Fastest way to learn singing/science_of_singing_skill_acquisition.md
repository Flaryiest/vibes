# Science of Adult Singing Skill Acquisition (absolute beginners)

Reading notes for the report writer:
- Verification tags on each finding: **[full text]** = I read the paper's own text; **[abstract]** = abstract or publisher/repository record only; **[secondary]** = reported by another paper, a lab summary, press coverage or a search-result summary, not checked against the original.
- Population tags: **adult novices**, **children**, **trained singers**, **clinical**, **non-voice** (limb/instrumental motor learning).
- "Cents": 100 cents = 1 semitone. The common research criterion for a "correct" note is within ±50 cents of target; "poor-pitch singing" is usually a mean error above 100 cents.
- Most recent work found: 2025 (Pfordresher & Greenspon; Nusseck et al.; Asadollahpour et al.). I found no 2026 controlled trial of singing training in adult beginners.

## 1. How common is true tone deafness versus ordinary untrained poor-pitch singing, and how trainable is adult pitch matching?

### Takeaway
True congenital amusia is rare (about 1.5%, up to about 4% on lenient criteria), whereas inaccurate singing in untrained adults is common and is mostly a production/sensorimotor-mapping problem, not a hearing problem. Cross-sectional evidence strongly suggests accuracy tracks singing experience ("use it or lose it"), but direct evidence of trainability in adults comes mainly from short, single-session experiments.

### Cited Findings
**Prevalence of amusia (strong: one very large sample)**
- Congenital pitch-based amusia prevalence is 1.5% (n = 214 of 16,625 adults analysed, from 20,850 online volunteers, 2008–2015); a lenient single-test criterion gives 4.2%. About 46% of first-degree relatives are similarly affected, and the deficit "is not attenuated by musical training". [abstract/full-text summary; general adults] — [Peretz & Vuvan 2017, Eur J Hum Genet](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5437896)
- Older estimate was about 4% (Kalmus & Fry 1980); a 2020 Colombian study of 383 students reported 0.52%. [secondary] — [Tillmann et al. 2023 review](https://lead.ube.fr/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/Tillmann_et_al._2023.pdf)

**Prevalence of poor-pitch singing (moderate: depends heavily on criterion and task)**
- In 79 college students imitating novel 4-note melodies, 10 (13%) averaged more than one semitone off and were classed as poor-pitch singers (8 female, 2 male); the abstract gives 10–15% across two experiments. In the same university, 59% of 1,105 intro-psychology students said they could not imitate melodies by singing, so self-belief far overstates actual deficit. [full text; adult novices] — [Pfordresher & Brown 2007, Music Perception](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Pfordresher&Brown_2007_MP.pdf)
- Those poor-pitch singers did not differ from good singers in pitch discrimination; they sang consistently sharp or flat (a transposition) and compressed interval sizes (80% of poor singers vs 15% of good singers had a compression slope ≤ .75). Authors attribute poor-pitch singing to "mismapping of pitch onto action", not perception, motor or memory deficits. [full text] — [Pfordresher & Brown 2007](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Pfordresher&Brown_2007_MP.pdf)
- With a ±100-cent criterion, 85–90% of the population sing "accurately"; with a stricter ±50-cent criterion, only 38% of 53 untrained adults matched at least 90% of pitches and 47% failed to match even 50% (Hutchins & Peretz 2012). [full text of the citing paper; adult novices] — [Demorest & Pfordresher 2015, Music Perception](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Demorest&Pfordresher_2015_MP.pdf)
- Largest standardised sample (Seattle Singing Accuracy Protocol online, N = 632, ages 6–99, 46% with no musical training): about half matched ≥ 90% of pitches within ±50 cents. Combining with a randomly recruited college lab sample (total N = 1,046), 37% were "accurate"; among those with no musical training, 24%. The authors' rough estimate: "about one third of the population may match pitch accurately the vast majority of the time". Under a mean-signed-error > 50 cents criterion, only 11% of the online sample would be "poor-pitch". [full text] — [Pfordresher & Demorest 2021, JRME](https://ubwp.buffalo.edu/apal-pfordresher/wp-content/uploads/sites/79/2022/01/PfordresherDemorest_2021_JRME.pdf)
- Same study: untrained participants averaged 68% of pitches correct vs 87% for those with private lessons (medians 78% vs 94%); untrained college-age lab participants averaged only 53% correct. Years of training correlated r = .30 with pitch matching; choir experience gave a further small advantage (87.6% vs 77.4%). All predictors together explained just over 25% of variance. [full text] — [Pfordresher & Demorest 2021](https://ubwp.buffalo.edu/apal-pfordresher/wp-content/uploads/sites/79/2022/01/PfordresherDemorest_2021_JRME.pdf)
- Dalla Bella, Giguère & Peretz (2007) tested 62 occasional singers on a well-known song; only a minority made many pitch errors and proficiency was roughly bell-shaped. [abstract/secondary] — [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17348539/)

**"Use it or lose it" (moderate: cross-sectional, one research group)**
- Mean absolute pitch deviation across three imitation tasks: kindergartners 109 cents (N = 77), sixth graders 51 cents (N = 55), college adults 92 cents (N = 78). Error rates (notes outside ±50 cents): 48.7%, 20.5%, 41.8%. Age effect on error rate F(2, 207) = 20.97, p < .001, partial eta squared reported as 0.1; sixth graders differed from both other groups, adults did not differ from kindergartners. [full text; children and adult novices] — [Demorest & Pfordresher 2015](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Demorest&Pfordresher_2015_MP.pdf)
- Adults by task: single pitch 66 cents (26% errors), two-note interval 87 cents (40% errors), four-note pattern 124 cents (54% errors). Using the ≥ 90%-correct criterion, 45% of sixth graders but only 5% of these college adults qualified. Musically inexperienced sixth graders (school music only) still beat inexperienced adults. [full text] — [Demorest & Pfordresher 2015](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Demorest&Pfordresher_2015_MP.pdf)
- Authors' conclusion: accuracy "may be related to variables involving singing experience rather than general development, and singing skill could decline over time if not maintained"; a footnote adds that males may have lost accuracy if they did not keep singing "as their voices mutated into mature male registers". [full text] — [Demorest & Pfordresher 2015](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Demorest&Pfordresher_2015_MP.pdf)
- Demorest's public summary: singing is a skill that can be taught and developed, more like practising an instrument than a fixed talent. [secondary; press release] — [Northwestern News 2015](https://news.northwestern.edu/stories/2015/02/cant-sing-keep-at-it)

**Limits: amusics respond poorly to generic training (weak: tiny samples)**
- Five adults with congenital amusia given a "broad-brush" singing intervention by a professional teacher; later papers cite it as about 7 weeks and as showing generic singing training is not an adequate remediation at that dose. [abstract + secondary; clinical] — [Anderson et al. 2012, Ann NY Acad Sci](https://research.gold.ac.uk/id/eprint/9926); [Jiang et al. 2023](https://reading-9.eprints-hosting.org/110788/1/Jiang_et_al_2023.pdf)
- A single-case 18-month vocal training programme in an amusic produced temporary gains without reaching normal levels. [secondary; clinical; the record appears to be Wilbiks et al. 2016] — [PubMed 28001646](https://www.pubmed.gov/28001646/)
- Eleven amusics (vs 11 controls) sang more accurately by imitation (after or in unison with a model), though pitch remained poor. [abstract; clinical] — [Tremblay-Champoux et al. 2010, Cogn Neuropsychol](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/02643294.2011.567258)

### Inferences
- An 18-year-old who has never sung is far more likely to be an "untrained sensorimotor mapper" than amusic (roughly 1–4% base rate). An app onboarding test should separate the two: a short pitch-discrimination check (SSAP uses an adaptive 2–4 minute test; thresholds above about 100 cents suggest a perceptual deficit) alongside a sung pitch-matching baseline.
- Expect a realistic starting point of roughly 65–125 cents mean error depending on task complexity (single note easiest, four-note patterns hardest), with about half of notes outside ±50 cents on short melodies. Starting there is normal, not a sign of low ability.
- Because accuracy varies so much with the criterion, the app should report a continuous measure (mean absolute cents, % of notes within ±50 cents) rather than a pass/fail label, and should test single notes, intervals and short patterns separately.
- The cross-sectional "sixth graders at about 50 cents after years of routine school singing" result suggests the attainable near-term target for an adult beginner is roughly 50 cents mean error on simple imitation tasks; this is an extrapolation, not a trial result.

### Gaps
- No longitudinal study follows the same people from childhood to adulthood, so "lose it" is inferred from cross-sectional comparisons of different cohorts tested with slightly different stimuli (children imitated a recorded female voice; adults a synthesised voice).
- I could not verify the exact percentages in Dalla Bella et al. (2007) or the full sample breakdown of Hutchins & Peretz (2012) from primary text.
- Whiteford & Oxenham (2018, Cortex) is cited by later papers as evidence that amusic pitch discrimination can improve with targeted training, but I could not access its abstract or numbers. — [record](https://scholarhub.vn/publication/Learning-for-pitch-and-melody-discrimination-in-congenital-amusia/01216473-0d71-4758-82bf-a46818a8532b)
- No data on singing accuracy for ages 12–18 or on how quickly accuracy decays without practice.

## 2. Does real-time visual pitch feedback speed learning to sing in tune, and are there downsides?

### Takeaway
Real-time visual pitch feedback that gives clear right/wrong information is the best-supported accelerator for beginners: it produced gains within a single 20-minute session where auditory-only practice did not. The evidence is contested over longer periods: a 10-week study found no advantage over practice without the display, performance can dip while watching the display, and gains can decay when it is removed.

### Cited Findings
**Supportive (moderate: several small controlled studies, mostly single session)**
- Adult poor-pitch singers were randomised to 20 minutes of (a) real-time visual feedback with auditory doubling, (b) auditory feedback only, or (c) a speech-imitation control, with SSAP pre/post tests. All groups improved somewhat, but only the visual group showed a significant training effect, with greater gains than either other group; the effect was largest for 4-note melodies rather than single pitches. Authors propose visual feedback substitutes for inaccurate auditory-motor associations. [abstract; adult novices] — [Berglin, Pfordresher & Demorest 2022, Psychology of Music 50(4)](https://researchconnect.buffalo.edu/en/publications/the-effect-of-visual-and-auditory-feedback-on-adult-poor-pitch-re/)
- A search-result excerpt of that paper states 94% of participants (n = 75) reported no singing training, implying a total sample of about 80. [secondary] — [SAGE record](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/03057356211026730)
- 47 complete beginners matched five pure-tone pitches with voice (and violin) in one session. Baseline voice error averaged about 271 cents (experts: about 27 cents). The real-time visual feedback group (with knowledge of results) improved by about 105 cents on a no-feedback transfer block; a group given a human-voice-timbre target improved about 121 cents while the aid was present but showed no retained gain (−11 cents at transfer); controls did not improve. Visual group exceeded the voice-timbre group at transfer (p = .022); Condition × Group η² = .29. [full-text summary; adult novices] — [Blanco, Tassani & Ramirez 2021, Frontiers in Psychology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8297736)
- Welch (1984) and Welch, Howard & Rush (1989; SINGAD): in children, visual feedback with knowledge of results plus varied practice improved pitch matching; visual feedback without knowledge of results did not. For melodies, accuracy often worsened during feedback but improved on later retention tests. [secondary; children] — [summarised in Blanco et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8297736)
- Wilson's University of Sydney thesis (basis of Wilson et al. 2008): visual feedback improved pitch accuracy; two display designs did not differ significantly; participants with some singing background improved more with the simpler display; cognitive load from decoding the display was a factor during the intervention. [abstract/secondary; adults] — [Sydney eScholarship record](https://ses.library.usyd.edu.au/handle/2123/1526)
- Wilson et al. (2008) and Leong & Cheng (2014) are reported as finding that participants given concurrent visual feedback improved singing accuracy more than those without after a training period. [secondary] — [White, IJRCS vol. 13 review](https://acda.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/IJRCSVol13White.pdf)

**Null or cautionary (moderate)**
- Ten weeks of practice with the game SingingCoach in non-music university students: both the concurrent-visual-feedback group and the control group improved significantly, but the feedback group did not score significantly higher. Participants' self-ratings of their singing rose. [abstract; adult novices] — [Paney & Tharp, Psychology of Music (2019 online / 2021)](https://scite.ai/authors/kevin-tharp-11lEWX)
- In that study, removal of concurrent feedback led to a decay in the experimental group's performance, to retention scores similar to controls. [secondary] — [White, IJRCS vol. 13](https://acda.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/IJRCSVol13White.pdf)
- Interviewed participants said the visual feedback demanded too much visual attention; the control group also lacked any knowledge of results. [secondary] — [Blanco et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8297736)
- Visual feedback without objective error information did not help non-musicians in Hutchins & Peretz (2012). In violin learning (Pardue & McPherson 2019) real-time feedback conditions did not differ and visual feedback demanded attention. [secondary] — [Blanco et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8297736)
- Poor-pitch singers imitated less accurately when the correct pitches were played along with them (augmented auditory feedback), whereas good singers were helped. [full text; adult novices] — [Pfordresher & Brown 2007](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Pfordresher&Brown_2007_MP.pdf)
- General motor-learning guidance for singing: frequent feedback can make students "dependent on feedback rather than their own intrinsic feedback", while reduced or faded feedback enhances retention; beginners on complex tasks may still need frequent, easy-to-understand feedback. [full text; narrative review drawing largely on non-voice research] — [Crocco & Meyer 2021, Journal of Singing](https://www.nats.org/_Library/JOS_On_Point/JOS_077_5_2021_693.pdf)
- A 2022 review argues real-time visual feedback should be a key approach in singing lessons. [secondary; I could not open the full text] — [Applied Sciences 12(21):10781](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/12/21/10781)

### Inferences
- The pattern across studies fits the guidance hypothesis: a display helps most when it carries unambiguous error information (how far, which direction, hit or miss), and its benefit is only proven when tested afterwards without the display. An app should therefore build "display-off" attempts into every exercise and score those as the real measure of progress.
- A sensible schedule is high-frequency visual feedback at first (the beginner lacks an internal reference), then faded: for example alternate display-on and display-off attempts, show the trace only after the attempt (terminal feedback), or reveal it only when the error exceeds a band. This fading schedule is extrapolated from motor-learning theory and has not been tested in a singing trial.
- Keep the display simple (one pitch line against a target band). Two independent sources flag cognitive load and visual attention as the main cost.
- Simply playing the correct note along with a struggling beginner may not help and may hurt; alternating listen-then-sing with a visual check is better supported than unison "sing along" for poor-pitch singers.

### Gaps
- I could not access Hoppe, Sadakata & Desain (2006, J Computer Assisted Learning 22(4):308–316), the original Welch, Howard & Rush (1989) paper, or Wilson et al. (2008); sample sizes and effect sizes for those remain unverified. — [DOI for Hoppe et al.](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2729.2006.00178.x)
- Paney & Tharp's sample size and effect sizes, and Berglin et al.'s exact N and effect sizes, were not available. A secondary source claims roughly a 20% accuracy gain in Berglin et al.; unverified. — [ScienceABC](https://www.scienceabc.com/humans/why-some-people-just-cant-sing-well-no-matter-how-hard-they-try)
- No study has tested faded or terminal visual feedback against continuous feedback in singing, and none has measured retention weeks after training in adult beginners.

## 3. What do motor-learning principles imply for voice training?

### Takeaway
Motor-learning principles (performance during practice is not learning; reduce and fade feedback; external focus; spaced practice; sleep) are widely recommended in voice pedagogy, but direct voice evidence is thin, small-sample and sometimes null. External focus of attention has the most consistent singing-specific support; feedback frequency and practice variability have mixed results.

### Cited Findings
**Performance versus learning**
- In-lesson performance "may be temporary and does not reliably indicate that learning has occurred"; learning must be inferred from retention (after practice stops) and transfer (to untrained variants). Teachers observed in studio lessons gave feedback on every attempt and spent little lesson time having students sing. [full text; narrative review, trained singers' lessons] — [Crocco & Meyer 2021](https://www.nats.org/_Library/JOS_On_Point/JOS_077_5_2021_693.pdf)

**Feedback frequency (mixed; small studies)**
- 30 adults learning a novel vowel-nasalisation task with 100%, 50% or no knowledge of results: higher relative feedback frequency gave poorer performance and learning, the 100% group being worst on accuracy and variability; a later review describes better 24-hour retention for the 50% group. [secondary, search-level summary of the abstract and of a Hong Kong University thesis review; healthy adults, voice task] — [Steinhauer & Grayhack 2000, J Voice, as reviewed here](https://core.ac.uk/download/38002934.pdf)
- Eight college voice students (ages 18–25) over a 15-week semester, alternating high- and low-frequency feedback: five retained less under high-frequency feedback, three retained more (those three were the higher scorers). [abstract; trained singers, single-subject design] — [Maxfield 2011 dissertation](https://iro.uiowa.edu/esploro/outputs/doctoral/Application-of-principles-from-motor-learning-theory/9983776919202771)
- Speech motor learning: summary feedback every 5th trial beat every-trial feedback at 2-day retention (n = 20 young women, Adams & Page 2000); but two later studies found no effect of feedback frequency (N = 60 older adults, p = .347; Lowe & Buchwald 2017). Authors conclude the ideal schedule for speech "remains unclear". [full-text summary; healthy adults, speech] — [Weir-Mayta et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8556735)
- Practical recommendation from voice pedagogy: many attempts per session with feedback on only some of them (high absolute, low relative frequency). [secondary; expert opinion] — [Maxfield, Journal of Singing columns](https://www.vocology.utah.edu/_resources/documents/taking_a_beat_maxfield.pdf)

**Focus of attention (most consistent singing-specific evidence, but subjective outcomes)**
- 30 untrained singers sang a three-note pattern under five attentional-focus conditions; a blind expert listener ranked tone quality higher under external focus (inter-rater reliability .84 on 20% of recordings). [abstract/secondary; adult novices] — [Atkins & Duke 2013](https://tdl-ir.tdl.org/handle/2152/21573)
- In 20 trained singers, ratings of ring and overall tone were higher the farther the attentional target was from the body; across four studies Atkins found better tone with external foci. [secondary; trained singers] — [Atkins, trained-singers study](https://www.metatoc.com/papers/92356-effects-of-focus-of-attention-on-tone-production-in-trained-singers); [review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8360264)
- Guidance: instructions should be simple, goal-related and external ("modify the vowel toward schwa") rather than internal ("raise your soft palate"); less instruction and sparing modelling are advised. [full text; narrative review] — [Crocco & Meyer 2021](https://www.nats.org/_Library/JOS_On_Point/JOS_077_5_2021_693.pdf)

**Practice variability and schedule (weak/null in voice)**
- A study of practice variability (three practice conditions; keywords include contextual interference and variable practice) for learning relaxed phonation found learning at delayed retention (lower sEMG) but no difference among practice conditions. Sources disagree on whether participants were vocally healthy or hyperfunctional. [secondary, search-level] — [Wong, Ma & Yiu 2011, J Voice, repository record](https://repository.hku.hk/handle/10722/125377)
- Training pitch matching across a full octave improved single-pitch matching in one session, whereas training across only a fifth did not (see section 6). [abstract; adult novices] — [Pfordresher & Greenspon 2025](https://researchconnect.buffalo.edu/en/publications/effects-of-pitch-range-on-singing-accuracy-training/)

**Spacing and sleep (non-voice evidence)**
- Musicians learning a keyboard sequence with rest intervals of 5 minutes, 6 hours or 24 hours: results suggest sleep-based consolidation improves accuracy, while speed gains come from both waking and sleep consolidation. [secondary; non-voice] — [Simmons, "Distributed practice and procedural memory consolidation in musicians' skill learning"](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/distributed-practice-and-procedural-memory-consolidation-in-musicians-skill-learning/813362932149649409-5172)
- Sleep after motor practice improved speed and reduced errors relative to equivalent waking intervals; contested by a study arguing the effect disappears when massed-practice fatigue and circadian factors are controlled. [secondary; non-voice] — [Walker et al. 2002](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC129381); contradicted by [PMC2921792](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2921792)
- Short rests interleaved with practice account for much early skill gain ("offline" gains during breaks); causation not established. [secondary; non-voice; news summary] — [Medical News Today summary](https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/amp/articles/why-taking-breaks-is-important-for-learning-new-skills)

### Inferences
- Design for many short attempts with self-evaluation before feedback: ask "was that sharp, flat or on?" before revealing the result. This targets the internal reference of correctness that reviews identify as the goal of feedback, and it is cheap to implement.
- Cue external targets (match the line, "send the sound to the far wall", copy the model's sound) instead of anatomical instructions. For a beginner, anatomical cues are also unverifiable without a teacher.
- Measure progress with next-day, display-off retention tests at the start of each session, not with end-of-session scores, which overstate learning.
- Mix exercise types and starting notes within a session rather than drilling one note repeatedly; the voice-specific evidence for this is weak, so treat it as a default borrowed from general motor learning rather than a proven accelerator.
- Daily practice (which the learner already plans) is consistent with the spacing and sleep literature; several short blocks per day are more defensible than one long block.

### Gaps
- No controlled study has tested blocked versus random practice, distributed versus massed practice, or sleep effects specifically on singing pitch accuracy.
- Focus-of-attention studies in singing measured rated tone quality on very short tasks, not pitch accuracy or long-term retention.
- I did not locate primary Verdolini or Titze experimental data on motor learning in novice singers; their influence here is via textbooks and pedagogy columns.

## 4. What is the evidence for semi-occluded vocal tract exercises and vocal warm-ups?

### Takeaway
Straw phonation, lip trills and humming have a plausible, well-described physical mechanism and are low-risk, but controlled outcome evidence is low quality, comes mostly from people with voice disorders, and shows mixed effects. Warm-up benefits are widely assumed and poorly demonstrated; neither has been shown to speed pitch-accuracy learning.

### Cited Findings
- Mechanism (expert account): a semi-occlusion raises pressure above the vocal folds, slightly separates them, lowers phonation threshold pressure and reduces collision forces, letting lung pressure and pitch be taken high in a glide with low contact stress; straw diameter matters most, length and material little. Titze states the exercises' efficacy "is no longer challenged". [full text; expert opinion/theory] — [Titze 2018, Journal of Singing 74(3)](https://vocology.utah.edu/_resources/documents/major_benefits_of_sovtes_titze.pdf)
- Systematic review and meta-analysis (Pozzali et al., J Voice; 8 studies, 7 pooled; people with dysphonia): versus control interventions, fundamental frequency and subglottic pressure improved, while shimmer, self-rated handicap and discomfort were significantly worse; no included study was at low risk of bias and evidence quality was very low. [secondary, search-level; clinical] — [repository record](https://air.unimi.it/handle/2434/870718); [ASHA Evidence Maps record A](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/99f029b3-9afb-ef11-8157-005056834e2b)
- Systematic review of straw phonation in dysphonic adults (Asadollahpour et al. 2025, J Voice): may improve glottal and acoustic measures, but studies were few, heterogeneous and at high risk of bias. [secondary, search-level; clinical; I could not confirm which of the two ASHA records corresponds to which review] — [ASHA Evidence Maps record B](https://apps.asha.org/EvidenceMaps/Articles/ArticleSummary/5baa3e22-e96e-4b14-a922-e848d530023f)
- Meta-analysis (2019): semi-occluded exercises significantly changed acoustic measures but not aerodynamic measures or contact quotient. [secondary] — [Kim 2019, Commun Sci Disord](https://www.e-csd.org/upload/csd-24-2-535.pdf)
- Systematic review in singers: phonation into glass tubes in water, straws and LaxVox tubes "promoted positive effects on the voice quality in singers". [secondary; trained singers] — [Mendes et al. 2018, J Voice (copy)](https://www.scribd.com/document/605177125/MENDES)
- Dose: in 69 healthy adults (35 women, 34 men) tested at 1 to 10 minutes of straw phonation, prolonging the exercise worsened voice quality and increased vocal effort and laryngeal discomfort. [secondary; healthy adults] — [PubMed 36722126](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36722126/)
- Low-level "controlled phonation" with a straw was a recommended recovery strategy after vocal exertion in healthy young adults, with a protective effect on a subsequent exertion task. [secondary; healthy adults] — [JSLHR 2021](https://pubs.asha.org/doi/10.1044/2021_JSLHR-20-00713)
- Warm-up: in 10 sopranos, a short submaximal warm-up raised phonation threshold pressure at high pitch (not obviously a benefit) and did not change it at comfortable or low pitch. Reviews describe aerodynamic effects of warm-up as inconclusive, with large between-subject variability, and note few studies exist. [secondary; trained singers] — [Motel, Fisher & Leydon 2003](https://read.qxmd.com/read/12825648/vocal-warm-up-increases-phonation-threshold-pressure-in-soprano-singers-at-high-pitch); [narrative review](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/3800833); [systematic review of warm-up/cool-down](https://www.scielo.br/j/rcefac/a/47rW5K8NFbCgJSsYD4hNY4K/?lang=en)

### Inferences
- A brief (about 1–3 minute) straw, lip-trill or hum glide block at the start of a session is a reasonable low-cost inclusion on mechanistic grounds and as a gentle way to explore pitch range, but the report should present it as "plausible and safe", not as proven. Keep it short: the one dose study suggests longer is worse.
- Semi-occluded glides double as a pitch-control exercise the app can track (a siren through the range gives the pitch detector clean signal and maps the learner's usable range), which is a design reason to include them independent of the vocal-health claims.
- There is no basis for a long mandatory warm-up routine for a beginner practising at moderate loudness in a comfortable range.

### Gaps
- No trial of semi-occluded exercises in vocally healthy adult non-singers with pitch accuracy, range or learning rate as outcomes.
- I found no pooled analysis of warm-up effects on singing accuracy or perceived effort in beginners.

## 5. Optimal session length and frequency, vocal fatigue and load limits: is 30–60 minutes a day appropriate?

### Takeaway
No study defines an optimal practice dose for untrained singers. Indirect evidence (fatigue after 60 minutes of continuous singing, slow recovery after 2 hours of loud voice use, benefit of rests and hydration) supports 30–60 minutes a day only if it is split into short blocks with rests rather than sung continuously.

### Cited Findings
- 21 female choir singers (mean age about 26.6) sang continuously for 60 minutes after a 10-minute warm-up: signs of vocal fatigue appeared (raised fundamental frequency, more self-reported symptoms), which improved after 30 minutes of silent rest. [abstract/secondary; amateur trained singers] — [Onofre et al. 2020, Eur Arch Otorhinolaryngol](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s00405-020-06238-7)
- 20 young amateur karaoke singers (10 male, 10 female, ages 20–25): those given water and short vocal rests at regular intervals sang significantly longer before fatigue than those singing continuously, and their voice quality and function did not change significantly. [secondary; abstract reproduced in a magazine piece; untrained adults] — [Yiu & Chan 2003, J Voice 17(2), abstract via Discover](https://www.discovermagazine.com/the-sciences/ncbi-rofl-effect-of-hydration-and-vocal-rest-on-the-vocal-fatigue-in-amateur-karaoke-singers)
- After 2 hours of oral reading at teaching loudness (72 teachers analysed), self-rated fatigue recovered about 90% within 4–6 hours and fully within 12–18 hours per the abstract (the paper's conclusion section states 50% within 4–6 hours and 90% within 12–18 hours); exponential time constant 3.7 ± 0.9 hours; larger gains between days suggest recovery during sleep. Authors advise limiting voice use when early fatigue symptoms appear. [full-text summary; occupational voice users, speech not singing] — [Hunter & Titze 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3311979)
- Straw phonation beyond a few minutes increased effort and discomfort in healthy adults (see section 4). [secondary] — [PubMed 36722126](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36722126/)
- Pedagogy recommendation that students should spend most of a lesson singing, with several attempts between feedback. [secondary; expert opinion] — [Maxfield column](https://www.vocology.utah.edu/_resources/documents/taking_a_beat_maxfield.pdf)
- Practitioner guidance (not research): start beginners at 15–20 minutes a day and build to 30–60 minutes; singing need not be continuous. [secondary; commercial blog citing a speech-language pathologist] — [30 Day Singer](https://www.30daysinger.com/blog/how-to-build-vocal-stamina)

### Inferences
- 30–60 minutes a day is defensible for an 18-year-old with a healthy voice, provided it is structured as roughly 10–20 minute blocks separated by rests (the only singing data show fatigue signs at 60 continuous minutes even in choir singers, and recovery within 30 minutes of rest). Starting nearer 20–30 minutes for the first weeks and building up is consistent with the practitioner guidance but not tested.
- Much of a pitch-accuracy session is not phonation: listening to targets, viewing feedback and listening back to recordings can make up half the clock time, which naturally spaces vocal load. The app can exploit this by interleaving listening/ear tasks between sung sets.
- Load depends on loudness and pitch height as well as time. Keeping early practice at conversational loudness in the comfortable range (section 9) minimises load; loud or high singing should be a small fraction of early sessions.
- The app should include a stop rule: hoarseness, throat discomfort, or loss of soft high notes ends the sung portion for the day. This follows the Hunter & Titze advice and is a safety default, not an optimised parameter.
- Splitting practice across two daily sessions also fits the motor-learning spacing evidence in section 3.

### Gaps
- No dosimetry or dose-response study in untrained adult singers; no evidence on optimal rest length between singing blocks. The vocal-dose literature itself poses "the ideal length and frequency of larger voice breaks" as an open question (search-level summary; I am not certain which of these two documents carries the wording). — [Hunter, ASA lay-language paper](https://acoustics.org/pressroom/httpdocs/153rd/hunter.html); [NCVS progress report](https://ncvs.org/archive/Final%20Progress%20Report%2020121128-for%20web.pdf)
- I could not obtain the exact singing durations per group in Yiu & Chan (2003).
- No study compares, for learning outcomes, one long daily singing session with several short ones.

## 6. How quickly do adult beginners measurably improve?

### Takeaway
Measurable pitch-matching gains appear within a single 20-minute session when clear visual feedback is given, and non-music students improve significantly over 10 weeks of regular practice, but there is no good longitudinal curve in cents for adult beginners over months. Range gains with training are documented only cross-sectionally.

### Cited Findings
- Single session, beginners matching pure tones: mean voice error fell by about 105 cents from a baseline of about 271 cents after practice with real-time visual feedback (retained on a same-session no-feedback block). [full-text summary; adult novices] — [Blanco et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8297736)
- Single 20-minute session, adult poor-pitch singers: significant SSAP improvement only with visual feedback, largest on 4-note melodies. [abstract] — [Berglin et al. 2022](https://researchconnect.buffalo.edu/en/publications/the-effect-of-visual-and-auditory-feedback-on-adult-poor-pitch-re/)
- Single session comparing training ranges: only the group that practised pitch matching across a one-octave range improved significantly, and only on single-pitch matching; a group trained across a seven-semitone range and an imagery control did not. [abstract; adult novices] — [Pfordresher & Greenspon 2025, Musicae Scientiae 29(2)](https://researchconnect.buffalo.edu/en/publications/effects-of-pitch-range-on-singing-accuracy-training/)
- Ten weeks: non-music university students practising with a singing game improved pitch accuracy significantly with or without concurrent visual feedback. [abstract] — [Paney & Tharp](https://scite.ai/authors/kevin-tharp-11lEWX)
- Two weeks of ear training alone (six sessions) did not improve singing (see section 7). [full-text summary] — [Zarate et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2887372/)
- Cross-sectional benchmarks: untrained adults 68% of notes within ±50 cents vs 87% for those with private lessons; sixth graders with routine school music about 51 cents mean error vs about 92 cents for college adults. [full text] — [Pfordresher & Demorest 2021](https://ubwp.buffalo.edu/apal-pfordresher/wp-content/uploads/sites/79/2022/01/PfordresherDemorest_2021_JRME.pdf); [Demorest & Pfordresher 2015](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Demorest&Pfordresher_2015_MP.pdf)
- Range: voice-range-profile studies show trained groups and more advanced singing students have wider pitch range (especially at the top) than untrained or first-year groups; these are cross-sectional comparisons. [secondary; trained singers] — [PubMed 20702062](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20702062); [Karger study of singing students](https://karger.com/fpl/article-abstract/68/2/53/141019)
- Commercial, non-randomised app data: a +6.1 percentage-point pitch-accuracy gain after four weeks; +5.9 points across 358 paired users, with the largest gains (+16.5 points, n = 70) for those starting below 75% accuracy. The vendor notes struggling users drop out, inflating the curves. [secondary; weak, vendor-reported] — [Singing Carrots blog](https://singingcarrots.com/blog/ai-singing-coach-7-months-who-improves-most/)

### Inferences
- A realistic expectation to communicate: noticeable improvement on simple note-matching within days, substantial improvement on short patterns within several weeks of daily practice, with the largest gains for those who start worst. Statements about specific cents-per-month rates would go beyond the evidence.
- Because the fastest documented gains come from the least accurate starters, a beginner's early progress curve will look steep and then flatten; the app should move the difficulty (longer patterns, wider intervals, display-off attempts, song phrases) to keep the measure sensitive.
- Single-session gains measured immediately are "performance", not proven "learning" (section 3); the app's own next-day retention data would be more informative than any published timeline.
- Range expansion should be presented as a slower, months-scale outcome with no reliable published rate for adult beginners.

### Gaps
- No multi-week randomised trial with retention testing in true adult beginners practising daily; no dose-response data; no published learning curve in cents over months.
- No longitudinal semitone figures for range gain in untrained adults; pages quoting precise figures (for example a "2.3-semitone gain over 18 months") had no traceable source and were excluded.
- No evidence on how far an adult beginner can ultimately go in pitch accuracy relative to trained singers (experts in Blanco et al. averaged about 27 cents on the same task).

## 7. Ear training versus vocal-motor control, model timbre, and comfortable range

### Takeaway
For most poor singers the bottleneck is translating a heard pitch into a vocal action, not hearing it; ear training alone did not improve singing in the one direct test. Beginners match a human voice in their own range (ideally a voice like their own) much better than piano or pure tones, although practising only with easy voice targets may aid performance more than lasting learning.

### Cited Findings
**Perception–production link**
- Non-musicians matched pitch correctly 97% of the time using a slider but only 59% of the time with their voice (musicians near-perfect on both). In a follow-up with 31 non-musicians: 2 (6%) had a perceptual deficit, 6 (19%) a vocal-motor control problem, and 11 (35%) could match the slider and their own recorded voice but not a synthesised voice (a timbre-translation problem). [secondary; lab's summary; adult novices] — [BRAMS summary of Hutchins & Peretz 2012](https://brams.org/2012/02/07/a-frog-in-your-throat-or-in-your-ear/)
- Follow-up using each participant's own recorded voice as the slider sound: participants were still more accurate with the slider than by singing; melody-singing ability correlated with vocal pitch matching but not with slider matching ("singing ability is rooted in vocal-motor control of pitch"). [secondary/abstract] — [Hutchins, Larrouy-Maestri & Peretz 2014](https://link.springer.com/10.3758/s13414-014-0732-1)
- Pitch discrimination correlates only modestly with singing accuracy: r = −.31 with pitch matching and −.21 with song singing (N = 632); earlier work found no significant difference in discrimination between poor-pitch and good singers. [full text] — [Pfordresher & Demorest 2021](https://ubwp.buffalo.edu/apal-pfordresher/wp-content/uploads/sites/79/2022/01/PfordresherDemorest_2021_JRME.pdf); [Pfordresher & Brown 2007](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Pfordresher&Brown_2007_MP.pdf)
- Direct test of ear training: 20 non-musicians (10 trained, 10 control); six sessions over two weeks of fine pitch ("micromelody") discrimination training improved discrimination but produced no group-by-time effect on single-note or melodic singing accuracy (melodic absolute error: trained 65.4 → 47.8 cents, control 78.2 → 76.7 cents, interaction not significant). Conclusion: "perceptual discrimination training alone is not sufficient to improve vocal accuracy in non-musicians." [full-text summary; small sample, so a modest benefit cannot be excluded] — [Zarate et al. 2010, PLoS ONE](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2887372/)
- Inverse-model account: 44 students (31 male, mean age 19) imitated recordings of themselves and of others. Everyone imitated their own earlier singing better, and the advantage was much larger for poor-pitch singers (112 cents better than for other-targets) than for accurate singers (39 cents); interaction ηp² = .12. The effect was not explained by self-recognition, timbre or absolute pitch. In the transposition experiment, error was lowest for non-transposed targets and highest for upward-transposed ones. [full text; adult novices] — [Pfordresher & Mantell 2014, Cognitive Psychology](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Pfordresher&Mantell_2014_CogPsy.pdf)
- Other proposed contributors not captured by standard tests: auditory imagery, pitch short-term memory, and a restricted pitch range during imitation that vocal limits do not explain. [full text / abstract] — [Pfordresher & Demorest 2021](https://ubwp.buffalo.edu/apal-pfordresher/wp-content/uploads/sites/79/2022/01/PfordresherDemorest_2021_JRME.pdf); [Pfordresher & Greenspon 2025](https://researchconnect.buffalo.edu/en/publications/effects-of-pitch-range-on-singing-accuracy-training/)

**Model timbre**
- 18 participants of varying ability sang back intervals from live piano, recorded piano, live voice and recorded voice models: matching was much more accurate for sung than piano models, live voice beat recorded voice, and participants who were out of tune with piano matched a live voice within accurate range (mean deviation 46.0 cents, SD 39.2); the worst singers gained most. [abstract; mixed-ability adults] — [Granot et al. 2013, J Voice](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/accuracy-of-pitch-matching-significantly-improved-by-live-voice-m/)
- The standard research protocol uses a recorded human model matching the participant's vocal range and male/female timbre category because "poor pitch singers and inexperienced singers do better when the timbre of the stimulus matches their own voice"; it adds a piano-timbre task to identify singers who cannot generalise to non-vocal timbre. [full text] — [Demorest et al. 2015, Music Perception 32(3)](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Demorest_etal_2015_MP.pdf)
- Children and adolescent uncertain singers sang less accurately in response to sine-wave models than vocal models. [secondary; children] — [Price et al. 1994 and Yarbrough et al. 1995, as reviewed by Hinkley 2021](https://acda.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/IJRCSVol9Hinkley.pdf)
- Caution: in beginners, a human-voice-timbre target improved matching by about 121 cents while present, but nothing was retained once it was removed, unlike visual feedback. [full-text summary] — [Blanco et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8297736)
- Singers shift toward a mistuned model: models mistuned by ±25 cents changed choir singers' intonation, flat models producing the greatest deviation. [full text; N = 76 trained choir singers] — [Hinkley 2021, IJRCS](https://acda.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/IJRCSVol9Hinkley.pdf)

**Comfortable range and task conditions**
- The Seattle protocol first finds a comfortable pitch (counting backwards from ten to capture speaking pitch, singing a familiar song unprompted, sustaining a comfortable "oo") and presents all targets within a fifth centred on it "to insure that accuracy problems are not a result of being asked to sing outside of a comfortable range". [full text] — [Demorest et al. 2015](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Demorest_etal_2015_MP.pdf)
- Task conditions appear to change measured accuracy a great deal: with a continuously sounding reference and unlimited time, 38% of non-musicians reached ≥ 90% correct; with a single hearing followed by immediate imitation, 5% of college adults did. These are different samples; the authors offer the methodological differences as the likely explanation and caution against treating the studies as equivalent. [full text] — [Demorest & Pfordresher 2015](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Demorest&Pfordresher_2015_MP.pdf)

### Inferences
- Core practice should be sung pitch matching with feedback, not passive ear training. Ear tasks (higher/lower, same/different, "was I sharp or flat?") are best used as a screening tool and as a rest activity between sung sets, tied to the learner's own output.
- Progression of target sounds suggested by the evidence: start with a male voice in the learner's own octave (or the learner's own best takes, re-used as targets, exploiting the self-imitation advantage); then synthesised voice; then piano/instrument tones; treat pure sine tones as the hardest case, not the default. The Blanco result implies easy voice targets should be paired with visual feedback and then with harder timbres so the gain becomes durable.
- Re-using the learner's own in-tune recordings as targets is a novel but evidence-motivated feature: poor-pitch singers were 112 cents more accurate imitating themselves.
- Start all targets within about a fifth around the learner's comfortable pitch, then widen toward an octave: the range-training study suggests wide-range practice is what transfers, but early success needs the comfortable zone.
- Early exercises can let the reference sound continuously and allow retries (conditions under which far more novices succeed), then move to hear-once-then-sing, which is closer to real singing.
- Reference models must be accurately tuned; learners drift toward a flat model.

### Gaps
- Only one small study directly tested whether ear training transfers to singing; no test of combined ear plus vocal training versus vocal training alone.
- The self-imitation advantage has not been tested as a training method over time.
- No adult-beginner study compares learning (not just immediate performance) across model timbres over multiple sessions.
- I could not verify primary numbers for Hutchins & Peretz (2012) or Watts & Hall (2008) on timbre.

## 8. Does self-recording and listening back improve singing accuracy or self-assessment?

### Takeaway
Self-assessment of singing is unreliable in both directions (many novices underrate themselves, professionals overrate), and listening back plausibly helps, but there is almost no direct evidence that self-recording improves singing accuracy. The one recent controlled study (pianists) found playback changed self-ratings without changing externally judged performance.

### Cited Findings
- Self-ratings track accuracy only moderately: agreement with "I am musically talented" correlated r = .41 with pitch-matching accuracy (N = 632); 14% of the 58 people most strongly denying musical talent were in fact accurate singers. [full text] — [Pfordresher & Demorest 2021](https://ubwp.buffalo.edu/apal-pfordresher/wp-content/uploads/sites/79/2022/01/PfordresherDemorest_2021_JRME.pdf)
- 59% of 1,105 students believed they could not imitate melodies by singing, versus 10–15% measured as poor-pitch. [full text] — [Pfordresher & Brown 2007](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Pfordresher&Brown_2007_MP.pdf)
- Professional sopranos rating recordings of their own and colleagues' "Happy Birthday" mostly overestimated their own pitch accuracy; the more accurate the singer, the more accurate the self-evaluation. [secondary; press release; trained singers] — [Max Planck Institute for Empirical Aesthetics on Larrouy-Maestri et al. 2021](https://www.aesthetics.mpg.de/en/newsroom/news/news-article/article/hitting-the-right-note.html)
- "Vocal generosity effect": listeners tolerate more mistuning in voices than in instruments, so by-ear judgement of sung pitch is lenient. [secondary] — [summarised in Blanco et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8297736)
- Controlled playback study: 25 advanced piano students either listened to a playback of their performance, listened and manipulated it, or read a book, then played again. Both playback groups rated their second performance higher on several dimensions, but 12 external raters found no significant difference between first and second performances. [full-text summary; non-voice, small sample] — [Nusseck et al. 2025, Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1568021/full)
- Prior literature as cited there: self-evaluation combined with listening to a recording increased instrumental performance (Hewitt 2001); self-listening had only a small, non-significant effect on self-evaluation accuracy (Summitt & Fisher 2016); student self-judgements agree less with experts than experts do with each other and improve with expertise (Hewitt 2005, 2011). [secondary; non-voice, school instrumentalists] — [Nusseck et al. 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1568021/full)
- Motor-learning guidance for singing treats building the learner's own error detection ("internal reference of correctness") as the central aim: ask the student to assess before giving feedback, and withdraw feedback over time. [full text; narrative review] — [Crocco & Meyer 2021](https://www.nats.org/_Library/JOS_On_Point/JOS_077_5_2021_693.pdf)

### Inferences
- Recording and playback is best justified as a calibration tool rather than a proven accelerator: pair every playback with the objective pitch trace so the learner learns what "30 cents flat" sounds like in their own voice. Unaided listening is likely to be lenient (vocal generosity) and biased by self-image.
- A "predict, then reveal" loop (learner rates the take, app shows measured accuracy) directly trains self-assessment and gives the app a measurable calibration score over time. This is an inference from the feedback literature, not a tested intervention.
- Saved in-tune takes can double as self-targets (section 7).
- Hearing one's recorded voice for the first time is often discouraging; the app's framing matters because self-perception was the strongest single correlate of accuracy in the large survey, and motivation is stressed in the motor-learning guidance.

### Gaps
- I found no controlled study of self-recording or playback on singing pitch accuracy in adult beginners. A 2016 Indiana University thesis examined self-evaluation, self-recording and modelling on second graders' singing accuracy, but I could not see its results. — [IU ScholarWorks](https://scholarworks.iu.edu/iuswrrest/api/core/bitstreams/2fd2fdb3-6ef8-46b1-bdcc-690629b6d670/content)
- No study quantifies how well untrained adults can judge the pitch accuracy of their own recordings against acoustic measurement.

## 9. Specific to an 18-year-old male with a deep voice

### Takeaway
At 18 the pubertal voice change is normally complete and speaking pitch has settled (about 120–130 Hz on average), but the vocal tract keeps growing until about 20–21 and laryngeal cartilages begin ossifying in the early twenties, so range and timbre may still shift slightly; none of this argues against training now. The main practical issue for low male voices is octave and timbre mismatch with reference models: male learners match male-voice models in their own octave best.

### Cited Findings
**Maturation**
- Voice mutation is most active between 12½ and 14 and usually complete by 15, lasting about 1½ years (up to 3); puberty overall completes between 13½ and 18 in males. The male voice drops about an octave, "averaging about 120 to 130 Hz by age 18 years". The vocal ligament is mature by about 16. [full text; clinical review] — [Sataloff & Kost 2020, Journal of Singing 77(1)](https://www.nats.org/_Library/JOS_On_Point/JOS-077-01-2020-63.pdf)
- The vocal tract "continues to grow in length and circumference through puberty and into adulthood. Full growth is usually not complete until age 20 or 21"; thyroid and cricoid cartilages ossify during the early 20s; respiratory muscular strength and stamina usually peak in young adulthood. The same review warns against working "heavily and too early" on exercises that stretch the extremes of range in young voices. [full text] — [Sataloff & Kost 2020](https://www.nats.org/_Library/JOS_On_Point/JOS-077-01-2020-63.pdf)
- Boys' voices begin to change at 12–13 and taper between 15 and 18; the voice matures around age 20 and then stays relatively stable until about 60. Male vocal folds grow roughly 0.7 mm a year over the first 20 years to about 16 mm membranous length. Lower pitches are more stable than upper ranges during change. [abstract-level; educational summary] — [National Center for Voice and Speech, "Voice Changes Throughout Life"](https://ncvs.org/?p=8649)
- Males may lose singing accuracy if they stop singing while their voices change into mature registers (authors' speculation). [full text] — [Demorest & Pfordresher 2015](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Demorest&Pfordresher_2015_MP.pdf)

**Octave and model effects**
- High-school singers responded more accurately to models of the same gender in the same octave (Williams 1994); among uncertain singers, males matched more often to lower-octave stimuli and females to upper-octave stimuli (Price et al. 1994); eighth-grade (changed-voice) males matched lower-octave stimuli more often whereas younger boys were more accurate with upper-octave stimuli (Yarbrough et al. 1995). [secondary; children and adolescents] — [reviewed in Hinkley 2021](https://acda.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/IJRCSVol9Hinkley.pdf)
- Adult research protocols avoid the problem by design: male participants imitate a male voice starting at C3 (131 Hz), an octave below female stimuli; targets span C3–G3, described as fitting the adult chest register. [full text] — [Pfordresher & Mantell 2014](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Pfordresher&Mantell_2014_CogPsy.pdf); [Demorest & Pfordresher 2015](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Demorest&Pfordresher_2015_MP.pdf)
- Researchers treat octave displacement as a choice, not a pitch error: when a participant sang in a different octave, targets were rescaled to that octave before scoring. [full text] — [Pfordresher & Brown 2007](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Pfordresher&Brown_2007_MP.pdf)
- When female participants had to imitate a male synthesised voice an octave away (first 20 participants of one study), the design was changed to give later participants same-octave models; of eight female poor-pitch singers, five had imitated the male voice and three a female voice. [full text] — [Pfordresher & Brown 2007](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Pfordresher&Brown_2007_MP.pdf)
- Imitation error was higher for targets transposed away from the original pitch, highest for upward transpositions. [full text] — [Pfordresher & Mantell 2014](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Pfordresher&Mantell_2014_CogPsy.pdf)
- Speaking pitch predicts comfortable singing pitch, which is why the Seattle protocol uses counting backwards from ten as one of three range-finding tasks. [full text] — [Demorest et al. 2015](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Demorest_etal_2015_MP.pdf)
- In one adult sample only 2 of 10 poor-pitch singers were male, and in the large amusia survey pitch-based amusia was, if anything, slightly more common in women (57.9% of cases) with no sex difference in test performance. I found nothing indicating low-voiced men are inherently worse pitch matchers. [full text / summary] — [Pfordresher & Brown 2007](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~pqp/pdfs/Pfordresher&Brown_2007_MP.pdf); [Peretz & Vuvan 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5437896)

### Inferences
- The app should measure this learner's comfortable pitch from his speaking and a sustained note, then centre early targets there. With a speaking pitch around 100–130 Hz (roughly G2–C3), a starting window around A2–E3 is more appropriate than the C3–G3 used for average male students; this specific window is my extrapolation from the protocol's logic.
- Reference tones and model voices should be presented in his octave with a male timbre. Most song recordings and many tutorials are pitched or sung higher than a bass-baritone's comfortable range, and female or child model voices sit an octave up; both add an octave-translation step that the evidence suggests is harder for inexperienced singers.
- Pitch detection must be octave-aware: score pitch class and report octave separately ("right note, octave below") rather than marking octave displacement as a 1,200-cent error, mirroring how researchers rescale targets. Separately, as an engineering consideration that does not come from the cited literature, low fundamentals (roughly 80–130 Hz) need longer analysis windows and are prone to tracker octave errors, so the detector should be validated on a bass voice.
- Songs will usually need transposing down (or singing an octave below) for a deep voice; a key-shift feature is a direct consequence of the comfortable-range findings.
- Voice type should be treated as provisional at 18. Range, especially the top, may still develop with growth until about 20–21 and with training, so the app should re-measure range periodically and avoid heavy early work at range extremes.

### Gaps
- No study specifically examines pitch-matching accuracy or training in adult low-voiced (bass/baritone) beginners, or octave-error rates in adult men; the octave findings come from children and adolescents.
- I found no peer-reviewed longitudinal data on fundamental frequency or singing range between ages 18 and 25. Claims that the male singing voice "fully matures" around 25–30 appear in pedagogy and consumer sources only and were not supported by anything I could verify.
- Whether a mismatched-octave model merely lowers immediate accuracy or also slows learning has not been tested.
